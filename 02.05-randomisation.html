Step 5: how to randomise

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page covers five randomisation designs and when to use each, and whether to run the draw in public or in private. It also explains how to check that the groups are comparable, the most common problems after the lottery, and how to pre-register the design.

With the feasibility conditions confirmed in Step 4, the next decision is how to randomise: which design fits the intervention, how the draw is run, and how to protect the comparison once it has been made.

Five ways to randomise your intervention

Not every intervention fits the same shape. The right design depends on your intervention’s structure, budget, and the learning questions you would like to answer. Here are five approaches in common use.

Lottery

How: randomly select which eligible units receive the intervention, by a documented public or private draw.

Best when: eligibility is clear, the intervention is oversubscribed, and a single treatment is being tested.

Multiple treatment arms

How: randomly assign units to different versions of the intervention (for example, grant only vs. grant plus advisory support), with or without a comparison group receiving nothing.

Best when: you want to know which design element drives results, not just whether the intervention works at all.

Randomised phase-in

How: everyone receives the intervention eventually, but in a randomly assigned order. Units still waiting serve as the comparison group in the meantime.

Best when: budget or capacity limits mean you cannot reach all eligible units at once, and withholding support permanently would be unacceptable.

Randomised intensities

How: vary the dose of the intervention across units: for example, 100%, 50%, or 0% of eligible employees receive training.

Best when: you suspect that knowledge or behaviour spreads between treated and untreated members within the same unit, and you want to measure that spread.

Encouragement design

How: randomise who receives extra outreach or information encouraging take-up, rather than randomising access to the intervention itself.

Best when: the intervention cannot legally be denied to anyone who qualifies (for example, an open voucher scheme), but take-up is voluntary and varies.

North‑East Romania used a lottery

North-East Romania used a simple lottery. The 1,038 eligible firms were split into two equal groups of 519 by a random draw. The treatment group was invited to apply for a grant of between €15,000 and €100,000 to invest in equipment, software, or services. The firms in the comparison group were not invited to apply and thus, received no grant. Both groups were surveyed in the same way throughout the evaluation period.

Public or private lottery?

Once you have decided to use a lottery, a second design choice arises: should the draw be conducted publicly or behind the scenes?

Public lottery

The draw takes place in front of an audience: for example, pulling firm names from a hat at a public event, or livestreaming the computerised draw.

Advantage: maximises transparency and public trust. Units can see for themselves that the process was fair.

Disadvantage: once comparison units know they did not receive support, they may change their behaviour (working harder to find alternative funding, for example), which can reduce the measured difference between the groups.

Private lottery

The draw is conducted internally by the evaluation team, with full documentation but without public observation.

Advantage: comparison units do not immediately know the outcome, which reduces the risk of behavioural reactions that narrow the gap between groups.

Disadvantage: the process is less visibly fair, which can make it harder to defend if challenged.

In practice, most RCT evaluations run the draw as a secure, documented, computerised procedure with a verifiable audit trail. This combines the rigour of a private draw with the accountability of one that can be independently checked afterwards.

Randomising only part of the pool: a lottery around a cutoff

In some interventions, it is not practical or desirable to randomise the entire eligible pool. A common alternative is to score all applicants on eligibility criteria, fund the top scorers outright, and reject the clear non-qualifiers outright. Only the units in the middle are randomised: those whose scores are close enough together that the difference is not meaningful.

This approach reduces the pool that is randomised, focuses the evaluation on the units where the decision is genuinely uncertain, and is easier to justify publicly than a lottery across the full range of applicants.

Keep the scoring process rules-based. Subjective assessment, even by expert panels, has been shown to introduce systematic bias. A structured scoring rubric, applied consistently, is fairer and more defensible than discretionary judgment.

Testing intervention versions: multiple treatment arms

A standard RCT compares one version of the intervention against a comparison group. But sometimes the real question is not whether the intervention works, but which design works best: grant only versus grant plus mentoring, for example, or light-touch advisory versus intensive coaching.

Multiple treatment arms allow you to test these versions against each other and against a comparison group in a single evaluation. Each group of units is randomly assigned to a different version, and outcomes are compared across all groups at endline.

The trade-off is sample size: more arms means more groups, and the available pool must be large enough to power comparisons between each pair.

When you cannot fund everyone at once: randomised phase-in

In many interventions, budget or implementation capacity means it is not possible to reach all eligible units in a single wave. A randomised phase-in turns this constraint into an advantage: units are randomly assigned to waves, and those still waiting to receive the intervention serve as the comparison group for the units already being treated.

The key advantage is that everyone eventually receives support, which makes the approach easier to justify politically and ethically. The key limitation is that there is no comparison group once the last wave has been treated, which means long-term impacts (those that take several years to emerge) cannot be measured.

Varying the dose: randomised intensities

Rather than randomising which units receive the intervention, you can randomise how much of it they receive. For example, some firms might have all eligible employees participate in a training intervention, while others have only half, and others none. Comparing outcomes across these groups reveals not just whether the intervention works but how much of it is needed, and whether the benefits spill over to employees who were not directly treated.

When take-up is low: encouragement design

Some interventions cannot be denied to anyone who qualifies: an open advisory service or a voucher scheme, for example. In these cases, it is not possible to assign units to a comparison group that is formally excluded from support. The encouragement design offers an alternative: rather than randomising access, randomise who receives extra outreach, information, or a targeted invitation to use the intervention.

Units who receive the encouragement are then compared with those who do not. Because encouraged units are more likely to take up the intervention than non-encouraged units, the comparison still produces an estimate of the intervention’s effect, even if both groups technically had access.

The main limitation is that the estimate reflects the effect of the encouragement itself as much as the intervention. If untargeted outreach (a news story, a social media post) reaches both groups, the comparison is diluted.

Compliance is rarely perfect in either direction: some encouraged units will never take up the intervention, and some non-encouraged units will find it and engage anyway. The right approach is to compare the whole encouraged group with the whole non-encouraged group, regardless of what individual units actually did. Your evaluation team will handle this in the analysis.

Did randomisation lead to comparable treatment and comparison groups?

Once the lottery has been conducted and the baseline survey fielded, the first analytical task is to check whether the draw actually produced two comparable groups. Even a well-designed lottery can, by chance, produce a treatment group that differs from the comparison group (e.g. on firm age, size, sector, or prior performance). A balance check compares the two groups on a set of baseline characteristics and tests whether any differences are larger than chance would produce. If the groups look similar before the intervention starts, the evaluation is on solid ground. If they do not, the analysis will need to account for the imbalance. What are potential challenges?

Even a well-designed RCT must survive contact with reality. These are the four most common problems, and what can be done about each.

Attrition. Units drop out between the baseline and the endline, e.g. because firms closed, relocated, or simply refused to participate in a survey again. If drop-out is higher in one group than the other, the two groups are no longer comparable and the impact estimate may be biased. Prevention is far easier than correction: collect multiple contact details at baseline, keep them updated during the intervention, and keep the endline survey as short as possible.

Spillovers. Comparison units benefit from the intervention indirectly: a treated supplier passes on improved products, or a treated neighbour shares what they learned in a training session. When this happens, the gap between the groups narrows and the evaluation underestimates the true effect. Spillovers are most likely in concentrated sectors or geographically tight clusters. Where the risk is high, randomising at a higher level (by region or sector cohort rather than by individual units) can contain it. If spillovers are expected, the RCT can be designed to measure them.

Romania (South-Muntenia): designing for spillovers South-Muntenia built spillovers into the design. About 900 applicant small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) are grouped into roughly 100 geographic or sector clusters. Each cluster is randomly assigned a funding intensity of 0%, 33%, 66% or 100%, and firms within each cluster are then randomly selected for funding up to that share. Comparing funded and unfunded firms within a cluster measures each firm’s own return; comparing clusters with different intensities measures the spillover onto neighbours and competitors. Read the full example.

Low take-up. Some units assigned to the treatment group never use the support: they decline the grant, fail to meet the conditions, or disengage early. Low take-up dilutes the estimated effect: if only half the treatment group actually received the intervention, the impact estimate reflects a half-dose. Monitoring take-up actively during implementation, and understanding why units do not engage, is essential for interpreting what the evaluation ultimately shows.

Uneven implementation. The intervention is delivered inconsistently across units, e.g. some firms receive more advisory time, faster disbursements, or better quality support than others. This introduces noise into the treatment and makes the evaluation harder to interpret. Clear delivery protocols, regular monitoring, and a close relationship between the managing authority and the delivery team reduce this risk.

None of these problems make an RCT impossible, but all of them must be anticipated at the design stage, not discovered after the endline survey.

North‑East Romania: the intervention did not achieve full take-up

Of the 519 firms selected by lottery, only 393 (75.7%) went on to submit a full funding proposal, and 333 (64.2%) were eventually contracted.

Because of limited take-up, it is safer to report the intention-to-treat effect, comparing everyone as originally assigned, even though it understates the effect on the firms that actually took up the grant.

Pre-register the RCT design before the intervention begins

Before the intervention begins (and at the latest before any data analysis begins), the RCT design must be publicly committed to on a recognised registry. This is called pre-registration, and it is one of the most important quality controls in a rigorous RCT.

Pre-registration means publishing, in advance, the primary outcome, the evaluation questions, the randomisation procedure, and the analysis plan. Once that record is time-stamped and publicly visible, no one can claim after the fact that a different learning question or outcome was always the primary focus. Nor can anyone claim that a subgroup analysis was planned from the beginning when it was actually prompted by the results. The evaluation team should lead this process, but the managing authority should confirm it has been done before approving the inception report.

For a managing authority, pre-registration also serves a protective function. It shields the evaluation team from pressure to adjust the analysis once results are known. And it strengthens the credibility of the findings: when a null result or an inconvenient conclusion is published, pre-registration demonstrates that it reflects reality rather than selective reporting.
