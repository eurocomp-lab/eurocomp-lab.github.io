Step 4: check whether an RCT is feasible

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page covers five feasibility conditions that must be in place before committing to a randomised controlled trial (RCT), why randomisation must be designed in from the start, and how to reach enough units for a credible evaluation.

By this point you have mapped your intervention’s causal logic, defined what you want to find out, and chosen the indicators you will use to measure it. The question this step answers is whether you can actually run a randomised evaluation to get those answers. How to design it follows in Step 5.

An RCT is not always possible. Before investing time and resources in a randomised design, a managing authority needs to check whether the basic conditions are in place, and whether enough units will take part.

Five conditions for a feasible RCT

Work through these five questions before committing to a randomised design. If the answer to the first three is not yes, an RCT is unlikely to be feasible and you should explore alternative approaches with your evaluation team.

1. Is the intervention oversubscribed?

An RCT requires more eligible applicants than available funding slots. If you can support everyone who qualifies, there is no basis for a lottery, and no credible comparison group. Oversubscription is the single most important precondition.

2. Is there enough time?

Randomisation must happen before the first grant is awarded. If the intervention has already launched, it is too late. Planning must ideally begin at the intervention design stage.

3. Is there a legal basis?

Most national frameworks were designed around ranking or first-come-first-served selection and say nothing about random assignment. Explicit permission, either in existing regulations or through an intervention-specific instrument, must be confirmed before proceeding.

4. Is there political and institutional buy-in?

An RCT requires sustained commitment from the managing authority and any intermediate bodies involved in delivery. If key stakeholders are not on board, or if political changes could interrupt the evaluation midway, the investment may not be recoverable.

5. Can the results actually be measured?

The indicators selected in Step 3 must be observable through administrative records, a survey, or both. If the main result indicator cannot be reliably measured for both treatment and comparison groups, the evaluation will not produce credible findings even if the design is sound.

Is randomisation permitted under your national legal framework?

Before designing the lottery or scoring mechanism, confirm that your national legal framework, and the specific legislation governing your intervention, either explicitly permits randomisation as a selection method or is flexible enough to accommodate it.

Most national frameworks were designed around ranking or first-come-first-served selection and say nothing about random assignment. A clear legal basis is the precondition for experimental evaluation to happen at all. See the legal framework page for country examples, key legal elements, and practical steps.

Randomisation must be designed in from the start

The most common mistake is treating randomisation as something that can be added once the intervention is already running. It cannot. By the time the first grant is awarded, the comparison group must already be defined, the eligibility rules must be locked in, and the baseline data should have been collected.

North-East Romania combined three steps that are usually separate (application, eligibility check, and baseline data collection) into a single online survey. This meant that by the time the eligibility pool was confirmed, the baseline data was already in hand. In October 2023, 519 of the 1,038 eligible firms were randomly selected to receive a grant. The other 519 became the comparison group.

Do you have enough units?

To detect the impact of an intervention, you need enough units in both the treatment and comparison groups. Too few units and even a real effect may be invisible: ordinary variation between units drowns out the signal. The technical term for this is statistical power, but the practical question is simple: is your eligible pool large enough?

The answer depends on how large an effect you expect, how much units vary on the main result indicator, and how confident you want to be in the findings. An evaluator will run a power calculation to translate these inputs into a minimum required sample. More details on power calculations

Lessons from EuroCOMP Lab projects point to several ways of reaching large enough numbers.

Invest heavily in outreach, especially to reach new beneficiaries

Standard outreach channels might fail to reach the units that the interventions are designed to serve. It is therefore worth investing in outreach and in testing different outreach approaches. Measuring which ones work can significantly expand the eligible pool and improve the quality of applications.

For interventions targeted to firms, standard outreach channels (newsletters, social media posts, agency websites) consistently fail to reach the firms that the interventions are designed to serve. Firms without prior experience of EU funding, smaller firms, and firms in rural or remote areas are systematically less likely to apply. This is not because they are ineligible, but because they do not know the intervention exists or lack the resources to navigate the application process. In this case, effective outreach might require direct, personalised engagement: in-person events, road trips to municipalities, collaboration with mayors and local business associations, and hands-on application support.

North‑East Romania

In North-East Romania, the World Bank randomised outreach activities across 46 cities, selecting 26 for road-trips where a consultant explained the call and helped firms complete the pre-assessment survey. The 26 cities that received road-trips produced 110 applications, against 71 in the 20 cities that did not: about 4.2 per city against 3.6. Applications from road-trip cities also came from significantly smaller firms (12.6 employees on average, compared to 19.6 in comparison cities). Compared to previous calls, the total number of applicants tripled and the number of eligible firms more than doubled. This small experiment demonstrated both the effectiveness of in-person mobilisation and the value of embedding experimentation into intervention implementation.

Eligibility criteria should remain broad enough

Eligibility criteria should not be so restrictive that too few units qualify, but they should remain specific enough to identify the units with the highest potential to benefit. Linking the criteria to the intervention’s Theory of Change keeps the wrong units out of the pool.

Romania (South-Muntenia)

In South-Muntenia (Romania), the World Bank found that eligibility criteria for an innovation support intervention were quite restrictive given the region’s firm landscape. The World Bank worked with the managing authority to adjust the criteria to target a higher number of firms.

Base intervention design on evidence of need

Interventions that are designed without a prior assessment of beneficiary needs frequently suffer from misalignment: either too few units apply (under-subscription), or the intervention attracts units that are not its intended target. Both problems are expensive, leading to repeated calls, unused funds, or support that reaches units that would have done well without it.

A baseline needs assessment, conducted before the intervention launches, allows the managing authority to understand the regional landscape, calibrate grant sizes and eligibility thresholds, and anticipate demand more accurately. Where uncertainty about demand remains, piloting different intervention elements before full-scale rollout can reduce the risk of costly mismatches.

Italy

In Italy, a minibond intervention pre-identified 289 eligible firms, of which only 30 applied: a hit rate of 10.3%. Of the 19 firms that ultimately issued a minibond, most had access to alternative financing and may not have needed the intervention at all. A prior needs assessment could have revealed that the intervention’s terms were less attractive than alternative instruments already available in the region, including zero-interest loans and non-repayable grants from earlier interventions. The design could then have been adjusted before launch.

Simplify the application process

Complex, document-heavy application processes disproportionately burden units that are least familiar with EU funding: the very units that interventions are often designed to reach. For example, when compliance requirements are similar regardless of grant size, small firms face the same administrative burden as large ones, often pushing them to hire consultants and creating a market for intermediaries that adds cost without adding value.

Application requirements should be proportionate to the amount of funding requested. Processes should be fully digital, integrated with existing administrative databases wherever possible, and capable of providing real-time eligibility feedback so that units can assess their likelihood of success before investing significant time in an application.

North‑East Romania

In Romania, the pre-assessment survey was designed to do three jobs at once, in approximately 40 minutes. It collected baseline data for the evaluation, assessed firm eligibility in real time, and gave firms enough information to decide whether to proceed. Survey responses were automatically cross-referenced with official firm data, enabling instant verification of core eligibility criteria. Firms that did not meet the requirements were informed immediately, saving them from investing further time in a formal application that would have been rejected.

Eligible firms received individualised benchmark reports comparing their digitalisation levels to similar firms in their region, providing added value to participants whether or not they were eventually selected.

Use the application process to exchange information

The application process is usually treated as a one-way flow of information, with applicants submitting data to the managing authority. In practice, it can also deliver useful information and tools back to applicants. Doing so can make applicants more engaged during the process and help selected units make better decisions about which activities to prioritise. It can also improve the experience of units that are not selected, who are more likely to view the process positively even without being selected.

For interventions targeted to firms, the application process can raise awareness of a specific topic (e.g. digitalisation, innovation, energy efficiency). It can also help firms identify investment needs and provide benchmarks that inform business decisions, whether or not the firm is selected for support.

Croatia

In the Scinergy pilot in Croatia, the World Bank supported an extensive outreach and information campaign. It included a dedicated project website, a LinkedIn campaign with paid promotions, appearances in television and online media, presentations at industry events and scientific institutions, and webinars targeting thousands of firms. The campaign was structured around a business-science matchmaking concept, providing firms with substantive information about how to collaborate with research institutions, not just how to apply for a grant. The initiative attracted widespread media coverage and generated strong interest in applications well before the call officially opened.
