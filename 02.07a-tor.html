Writing terms of reference for an RCT

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page sets out how to write Expression of Interests (EoI) and what the Terms of Reference (ToR) must contain: intervention background, learning questions, scope of work, methodology, deliverables, budget, data access, required qualifications, and common mistakes to avoid.

Managing authorities bring deep knowledge of their interventions to the table; the technical expertise needed to evaluate them rigorously can be contracted in. While this website provides the tools and guidance to get started, implementing randomised controlled trials (RCTs) comes with many specificities and challenges, and having the right expert team on board makes all the difference. What managing authorities need is a well-crafted ToR: the document that attracts the right teams, sets the methodological bar, and defines the conditions for a credible, policy-relevant evaluation. A strong ToR is therefore not a procurement formality: it is an important contribution of the managing authority to the quality of the evaluation.

In principle, a ToR can cover the entire evaluation, including evaluation design, Theory of Change (ToC) development, and selection of the methodological approach, leaving these decisions to the consultant. However, since this website focuses specifically on RCTs as the evaluation design of choice, it assumes that managing authorities have already completed the preparatory steps covered on the previous pages. The ToC is in place, the evaluation questions and indicators are defined, the feasibility of an RCT has been confirmed, the randomisation design has been chosen, and the available data sources have been mapped.

The ToR therefore focuses on two specific functions: preparing and implementing data collection, and conducting the statistical analysis. These can be covered by a single ToR commissioning one consultant or team, or split across two separate ToRs: one for an evaluation consultant responsible for analysis, and one for the data collection firm responsible for fieldwork.

Part 1: the procurement process

Step 1: issue an EoI

For technically complex evaluations, it is strongly recommended to begin with an EoI rather than going directly to a full ToR. An EoI is a short, non-binding call that invites qualified organisations to signal their interest and demonstrate their eligibility before the full competition begins.

The EoI serves three purposes. First, it maps the market: the pool of RCT-capable evaluation teams is narrower than the general consulting market, and an EoI quickly identifies who is actually available and qualified. Second, it allows the managing authority to shortlist candidates who are then invited to submit full proposals in a restricted procedure, which is more efficient for both sides. Third, EoI responses often reveal market realities (realistic timelines, typical costs, data access constraints) that can be incorporated into the final ToR.

An EoI should contain:

  • A brief description of the intervention and the intended evaluation approach
  • The estimated budget range and timeline
  • Shortlisting criteria: relevant experience, team qualifications, organisational capacity
  • What to submit: a short letter (maximum 3-5 pages), CVs of key staff, two to three examples of comparable prior evaluations
  • Submission deadline and contact point

The EoI should be open for a minimum of two to three weeks and published through the national procurement portal and the managing authority’s website. Shortlisted candidates, typically three to six, are then invited to submit full proposals in response to the complete ToR.

Step 2: issue the ToR and evaluate proposals

With shortlisted candidates identified, the managing authority issues the full ToR. Proposals are evaluated against pre-defined, published criteria. For an RCT evaluation, technical quality should be weighted heavily:

Criterion Suggested weight
Technical approach and methodology 40%
Relevant experience and past performance 25%
Team qualifications 20%
Price 15%

A low-bid selection for a technically complex evaluation is a false economy. The proposal panel should include at least one member with methodological knowledge of RCTs, or an external technical reviewer where this expertise is not available internally.

Before making a final selection, shortlisted candidates should be invited to a clarification interview. Useful questions include: how would you handle lower-than-projected take-up? At what level of attrition would you consider the evaluation compromised?

Step 3: contract signature and inception

Once the contract is signed, the evaluation begins. The first milestone is the inception report, in which the consultant refines the ToC, finalises the evaluation design, confirms the sampling and randomisation strategy, and presents survey instruments for review. This is the last point at which fundamental design decisions can be changed without significant cost or delay.

Key questions to assess at inception review:

  • Is the power analysis based on realistic assumptions about take-up, response rates, and effect sizes?
  • Is the randomisation strategy robust to the specific implementation context?
  • Has the evaluation been pre-registered, or is it about to be?
  • Are all data access arrangements confirmed?

Part 2: what the ToR must contain

1. Background, intervention description, and ToC

This section gives the consultant the context needed to design the data collection instruments and analysis plan. Include:

  • Name and objectives of the intervention and funding instrument
  • Description of the intervention: what participants receive, how much, under what conditions
  • Eligibility criteria and selection methodology
  • Total budget, value of individual awards, and number of expected beneficiaries
  • Implementation timeline and any prior rounds
  • Relevant regulatory obligations (e.g. applicable state aid rules)

Include the completed ToC, covering inputs, activities, outputs, short- and medium-term results, impacts, and assumptions. The ToC has been developed by the managing authority and is provided to the consultant as a fixed reference point for designing the measurement framework and survey instruments.

2. Learning questions

List the primary and secondary learning questions explicitly. These have been defined by the managing authority and are provided to the consultant as the analytical framework that the data collection and analysis must address. State the intended use of the findings.

3. Scope of work

The consultant’s responsibilities cover two functions: data collection and analysis. Specify clearly which tasks fall under each and, where a separate data collection firm is contracted, which tasks are covered elsewhere.

Data collection:

  • Finalise baseline and endline survey instruments based on the ToC and evaluation questions provided
  • Pilot the questionnaire and propose revisions
  • Oversee or coordinate fieldwork; supervise the data collection firm where one is separately contracted
  • Implement randomisation and produce a balance report confirming comparability of treatment and control groups
  • Design and maintain a monitoring framework tracking take-up, compliance, contamination, and attrition

Analysis:

  • Clean and merge survey and administrative data
  • Conduct impact analysis and heterogeneity analysis
  • Produce draft and final evaluation reports and a non-technical documents (e.g. a policy brief)
  • Present findings to the managing authority and relevant stakeholders

4. Methodology

The methodology section is the technical core of the ToR. It should specify the evaluation design clearly enough that a qualified evaluator can assess whether the approach is appropriate, without being so prescriptive that it prevents the consultant from bringing their own expertise to bear.

Design: Specify that the evaluation will use an RCT, and define the unit of randomisation (for business support, typically the firm), the level of randomisation (individual or cluster), and whether there are multiple treatment arms.

Sample size and power: Require a formal power analysis during the inception phase. The consultant must specify and justify the minimum detectable effect, required statistical power, significance level, expected take-up and attrition rates, and baseline variance of the main result indicator.

Estimation: The main analysis should compare all units offered the support with all units in the comparison group, whether or not the units offered actually took the support up. This keeps the comparison fair and shows the effect of offering the intervention. If some of them did not take it up, the consultant should also estimate the effect on those that actually received it.

Pre-registration: Require pre-registration on a recognised public registry, such as the American Economic Association (AEA) RCT Registry, before any data is collected. The pre-registration record must include the research question, randomisation procedure, main and secondary result indicators, and analysis plan.

Data sources: Specify which administrative datasets will be provided by the managing authority and which the consultant must access independently. If primary survey data is needed, clarify whether survey fieldwork is in scope or covered by a separate data collection contract.

5. Deliverables and timeline

Deliverable Review period
Inception report: refined ToC, evaluation design, power calculations, survey instruments 15 working days
Baseline report: survey data, balance checks, randomisation confirmation 15 working days
Monitoring reports: take-up, compliance, attrition 10 working days
Draft endline report: impact analysis, heterogeneity analysis 20 working days
Final evaluation report 10 working days
Policy brief: 4–6 page non-technical summary 10 working days
Presentation of findings As agreed

6. Budget

Why a precise estimate is difficult. The cost of an RCT depends on many design choices specific to each intervention. The table below shows the main variables that define the budget and how each one affects it.

Variable How it affects the budget
Sample size More participants mean higher survey costs, because each additional interview adds fieldwork cost.
Design complexity Each additional treatment arm or planned subgroup comparison requires more participants. Randomising in clusters, such as regions or sectors, rather than individual units also requires a larger sample to detect the same effect.
Number of survey rounds Each round adds a full fieldwork cycle: updating the questionnaire, training enumerators, conducting interviews, and cleaning data. Because data collection is usually the largest cost item, adding a midline or a second follow-up raises the total considerably.
Data collection mode Web surveys are the cheapest per interview, telephone interviews (CATI) cost more because of interviewer time, and face-to-face interviews (CAPI) cost the most because of travel and enumerator time. Cheaper modes may need extra follow-up to keep response rates up.
Questionnaire length and languages Longer questionnaires increase interviewer time per interview for CATI and CAPI, and can lower response rates. Each additional language adds translation, back-translation, and piloting costs.
Geographic spread A widely dispersed sample increases travel time and costs for face-to-face interviews. It makes little difference for web or telephone surveys.
Use of administrative data Lowers costs where tax, registry, or social security records can replace survey rounds, especially the follow-up. Some costs remain for negotiating access, data-sharing agreements, linking and cleaning the data, and sometimes access fees.
Duration of the evaluation A longer evaluation means more months of research team time and project management, and more effort to keep track of participants between rounds. RCTs often run several years from design to final report.
Team composition and national labour costs Senior researchers cost more per day than junior staff, and fees for enumerators and consultants vary widely across countries.
Reporting and dissemination Workshops, translated reports, policy briefs, and presentations add relatively modest costs, mainly staff time and travel.

Indicative orders of magnitude. International benchmarks give a sense of scale. In a sample of impact evaluations supported by the World Bank’s Strategic Impact Evaluation Fund, costs ranged from about USD 130,000 to USD 2.8 million, with an average of around USD 1 million. A review of 89 impact evaluations funded by 3ie found an average budget of about USD 344,000, and about USD 374,000 for RCTs. In both cases, new data collection was the largest single cost, typically 60–65% of the total. Most of these evaluations took place in low- and middle-income countries. Fieldwork and staff costs in the EU are generally higher, although good administrative data can reduce the need for primary surveys.

As a rough guide for EU competitiveness interventions:

Scope Indicative range
Administrative data only; no primary survey €100,000 – €250,000
Baseline and one follow-up survey; a few hundred firms €250,000 – €600,000
Multiple survey rounds, cohorts, or treatment arms; larger samples €600,000 – €1,000,000 or more

These ranges are indicative only. Use the responses to the EoI (Part 1) to check them against current market prices before setting the envelope. The budget should be set before the ToR is issued, not derived from what remains after other costs. The ToR should state explicitly whether fieldwork costs are included in the evaluation budget or procured separately.

Budget structure. Ask applicants to break down their financial proposal by task: inception and design; baseline data collection; monitoring; endline data collection; analysis; reporting and dissemination; travel; and contingency (typically 5–10%).

Payment schedule. Payments should be tied to approved deliverables, not to the passage of time:

Payment Trigger Share
1st Contract signature 10%
2nd Approval of inception report 20%
3rd Approval of baseline report 25%
4th Approval of draft endline report 30%
5th Approval of final report and policy brief 15%

Specify clearly whether the budget is inclusive or exclusive of Value Added Tax, and whether travel and data access costs are included or reimbursable separately.

7. Data access and GDPR

Specify which datasets the managing authority will provide, in what format and timeline. The ToR must address General Data Protection Regulation (GDPR) compliance (lawful basis for processing, pseudonymisation, data security requirements, and retention obligations) and require a data processing agreement to be signed before any data transfer.

8. Qualifications required

For a full RCT evaluation, require:

  • an advanced degree in economics, statistics, or a related quantitative field
  • demonstrated experience designing and implementing experimental impact evaluations (with publicly available outputs as evidence)
  • experience with data on the target population (for business support, firm-level data)
  • familiarity with EU cohesion policy and state aid rules

Where a team is required, specify the expected composition and qualifications for each role.

9. Governance and proposal requirements

Describe the reporting line, frequency of progress meetings, and the composition of any evaluation steering committee. Specify the escalation procedure for methodological disagreements. State what applicants must submit (technical proposal length, CV format, number of reference evaluations, financial proposal format) and the submission deadline.

Common mistakes to avoid

  • Starting too late. An RCT must be designed before beneficiaries are selected. Procurement should begin at least six to twelve months before the first grant is awarded.
  • Leaving the design entirely to the consultant. The ToR must include a preliminary ToC and evaluation questions: a consultant who receives no guidance will measure what is easy, not what matters.
  • Setting an unrealistic budget. A ToR with an insufficient budget envelope will either attract low-quality proposals or result in a scope reduction that compromises the evaluation.
