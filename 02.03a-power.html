Power calculations: how many firms you need

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7

Summary

This page explains what a power calculation is, what goes into it, and what a Managing Authority needs to know to have a useful conversation with an evaluator about sample size.

A power calculation sounds technical. In practice it answers one practical question: do you have enough firms to detect whether the programme works? This page explains what drives that number, how to think about it without needing to do the maths yourself, and what to do if the answer turns out to be no.

The core problem: too few firms and you may miss the truth

Imagine a programme that genuinely improves firm productivity by 8%. If your evaluation includes only thirty firms, the natural variation between those firms - some were already growing fast, some were already struggling - may easily produce an 8% difference between treatment and comparison groups by chance alone. You cannot tell whether the programme caused the difference or whether chance did. The programme may work, but your evaluation cannot prove it.

Add more firms and the picture becomes clearer. With enough observations, the average outcomes in the two groups settle down and genuine differences become visible. The variation that masks the signal shrinks relative to the size of the groups.

Too few firms

Real impacts may go undetected: ordinary differences between firms drown out the programme’s effect.

Enough firms

Real impacts become measurable: averages settle and the difference stands out.

More firms do not make the lottery fairer - they make its answer visible.

What drives the required sample size?

The number of firms you need depends on two things that pull in opposite directions.

The size of the effect you want to detect

A programme that doubles firm turnover is easy to spot even in a small group. A programme that increases turnover by 5% requires a much larger group to detect reliably.

Small expected effect → more firms needed. Large expected effect → fewer firms needed.

How much firms vary from each other

If all firms in the target population are very similar - same size, same sector, similar starting performance - a small sample is enough to pick up a difference. If firms vary enormously, you need more of them to be confident that a difference reflects the programme rather than the mix of firms you happened to include.

High variation → more firms needed. Low variation → fewer firms needed.

Think of trying to hear a quiet voice in a noisy room. A louder voice needs no particular conditions to be heard. A quieter voice needs a quieter room - or more people listening carefully. More firms are the equivalent of reducing the noise.

The four ingredients a power calculation combines

A power calculation takes four inputs and returns a single number: the minimum sample size the evaluation needs. The evaluator handles the calculation; the Managing Authority needs to understand what the inputs mean so that the conversation about sample size is grounded in the programme’s actual context.

Here is what each ingredient means in plain language:

Expected effect. What is the smallest improvement in your primary outcome that would be meaningful enough to be worth detecting? This is a policy judgement, not a statistical one. If a 5% increase in firm R&D spending would be considered a success, that is your expected effect. If only a 25% increase would change how the programme is designed in the next round, use that. Evidence from similar programmes in other regions or member states can help anchor this number. The smaller the effect you want to reliably detect, the more firms you will need.

Variation between firms. How much do firms in your target population differ from each other on the outcome of interest, even before the programme starts? This is usually estimated from existing data - a prior round of the programme, national statistics, or a comparable survey. The evaluator will handle this, but it is worth knowing that the more heterogeneous your target population, the larger the required sample.

Confidence level. How willing are you to risk concluding that the programme worked when it actually did not? The standard is a 5% risk - meaning the evaluation is designed so that a false positive would only arise by chance in 1 out of 20 identical studies. This does not normally need to be changed.

Statistical power. How willing are you to risk missing a real effect? The standard is 80% - meaning the evaluation has an 80% chance of detecting a genuine effect if one exists, and a 20% chance of missing it. For evaluations where a null result would have major consequences - for example, if it would be used to cancel a programme - 90% power is preferable, but requires a larger sample.

North‑East Romania: Was 1,038 companies enough for North‑East Romania? Once the power calculation is run, you compare the result against the size of your eligible pool. If your pool exceeds the required sample, you are ready to randomise. If it falls short, you need to grow the pool before the draw.

In North-East Romania, the evaluation team ran the power calculation before the draw and confirmed that the pool of 1,038 firms was large enough to detect the effects they expected - which is why the draw proceeded with 519 firms in each group.

Always randomise more firms than you need at endline

The power calculation tells you how many firms you need to observe at the end of the evaluation - at the endline survey. But not every firm randomised at baseline will still be reachable by then. Some will have closed, relocated, or simply stopped responding. This is called attrition, and it is one of the most common threats to the validity of an RCT.

The practical implication is straightforward: always recruit more firms into the randomised pool than the power calculation requires, to leave a buffer for expected drop-out. If you need 400 firms at endline and expect that roughly one in five will be unreachable by then, you need to randomise at least 500 firms at baseline.

The evaluation team will advise on the right buffer based on comparable studies, the expected follow-up period, and the availability of administrative data - which, in the EU, can often be used to track firms that drop out of surveys.

Attrition is not just a power problem. If firms drop out at different rates in the treatment and comparison groups - for example, if struggling firms in the comparison group are more likely to close - the two groups stop being comparable and the impact estimate becomes biased. Step 7 covers how to detect and address this.

What if there are not enough firms?

If the power calculation reveals that your eligible pool is too small, there are six practical fixes. The right combination depends on what is driving the shortfall.

Increase outreach

More applicants mean a larger eligible pool. North-East Romania ran outreach events in more than 20 cities before the application window opened, specifically to ensure the pool would be large enough.

Expand eligibility

Lowering the eligibility threshold adds more firms to the pool. Be careful not to expand so broadly that the evaluation no longer answers the original question - the expanded population should still be the one the programme is designed to serve.

Combine regions or cohorts

If a single region or programme round does not produce enough firms, pooling two regions running similar programmes - or two consecutive cohorts - into a single evaluation can reach the required sample without changing the design.

Run a phase-in

Rather than running the programme to all eligible firms at once, stagger rollout across waves. Each wave that is still waiting acts as the comparison group for the waves already receiving support - doubling the effective sample without needing more firms overall.

Focus on where effects are largest

If the expected effect is larger among a specific subgroup - younger firms, firms in a particular sector, firms with no prior R&D experience - narrowing the focus raises the detectable effect and reduces the required sample. This only makes sense if the narrowed population remains policy-relevant.

Invest in a strong baseline

Collecting good baseline data on the primary outcome - before the intervention - and using it in the analysis significantly reduces the sample size needed. The evaluator can explain how much this helps in your specific context; the reduction is often substantial.

A practical note for Managing Authorities

The power calculation is usually the evaluator’s job - not the Managing Authority’s. But the Managing Authority is the only party that can answer the policy question that drives the most important input: what effect would be large enough to matter?

A Managing Authority that can say “we would consider the programme successful if it increased firm R&D spending by at least 15%” gives the evaluator something concrete to work with. A Managing Authority that leaves that question open will get a power calculation based on assumptions it may not recognise or agree with - and may end up with a required sample that is either impossibly large or so small that meaningful effects cannot be detected.

The conversation about sample size is one of the most important early conversations between the Managing Authority and the evaluation team. Have it before the programme design is finalised - not after the eligible pool is already fixed.

Tools for power calculations

The evaluator will run the power calculation, but free tools are available if you want to explore the numbers yourself or check the evaluator’s assumptions.

IDÉE Power Calculator

Web-based, no software required, designed for non-specialists. A good starting point. idee-education.fr

EGAP Power Guide

A plain-language explanation of power concepts with an interactive calculator. egap.org

World Bank DIME Wiki

Step-by-step guidance with worked examples drawn from real evaluations. dimewiki.worldbank.org

G*Power

Free software covering a wide range of experimental designs psychologie.hhu.de
