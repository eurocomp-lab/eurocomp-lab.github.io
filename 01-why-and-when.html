Why and when to use a randomised controlled trial

Summary

This page explains in more detail why experimentation is worth doing and when to plan it. It explains what impact evaluation is and why experimentation - running a randomised controlled trial (RCT) - is usually the preferred way to do it, and when to use one: which questions it can answer, why it has to be planned early, and when it is not the right tool.

What is impact evaluation for?

Monitoring and evaluation (M&E) vs impact evaluation

M&E

Collect data on inputs, outputs, outcomes and participants to

  • Improve day-to-day management and decisions
  • Make descriptive before and after comparisons

Describes what is happening, but not whether it is because of the intervention.

Impact evaluation

Collect data on participants and comparison groups to

  • Find the causal impact of a programme
  • Find the most effective way to deliver a programme
  • Support cost-benefit analysis

Determines impact in a way that can only be attributed to the programme, by establishing what would have happened without it.

Monitoring and impact evaluation are complements, not alternatives. An impact evaluation should not go ahead without solid monitoring data: who applied, who was selected, who was contracted, and what they invested in. Without that, you cannot tell whether a programme had no effect or was simply never implemented as planned.

What exactly is the impact of a programme?

Definition. Impact is what happens after a programme is introduced, minus what would have happened in its absence, the counterfactual.

The dilemma. The counterfactual is a “what if” and is unknown to us. Ideally, we would observe the same applicant twice, once with the programme and once without it, and compare. That is impossible: each applicant either takes part or does not. Every impact evaluation method is a way of approximating that missing second observation.

The solution. Find a way to construct the counterfactual so impact can be measured. This can be done in different ways. The page shows different methods of constructing the counterfactual and explains why RCTs are considered the gold standard.

Impact is measured using outcomes

When evaluating projects, often only outputs are assessed, because they are the easiest thing to count. Measuring impact means moving from counting outputs to measuring outcomes.

Outputs

What a programme delivers. For example, grants disbursed, firms enrolled, training sessions run.

Outcomes

What changes because of it. For example, growth, jobs, innovation, resilience.

The impact in the North‑East Romania programme

For North-East Romania, the impact is the gap between treatment and comparison firms on four tracked outcomes: digital adoption, revenue, employment, and productivity.

Before implementation, map out how a programme’s activities are expected to change the problem you are addressing. That shows, in advance, why each step should lead to the next. That causal logic, the how and why behind each step, explains a programme’s reason for existing. It also tells you what you need to measure in order to test whether the expected change is actually happening.

But which outcomes should one look at exactly? A programme can change many things, and not all of them can or should be measured. To know which outcomes to look at, and when to expect them to change, start by spelling out how the programme is supposed to work: how its inputs and activities lead to outputs, and how those outputs are expected to lead to outcomes.

This chain is the foundation of a theory of change, and having thought it through helps in designing a strong impact evaluation. There is more on this in Step 1: develop a Theory of Change.

Making the chain explicit also surfaces the assumptions behind each link, and shows which of them the programme’s impact depends on most. If one of these assumptions does not hold (for example, that firms will take up the support they are offered), the programme may fall short of its intended impact. One way to reduce this risk is to test, within the same evaluation, alternative ways of addressing that weak link, such as two different outreach approaches, and see which works better.

Digitrans example The Digitrans pilot (see Examples for more details) tested and compared two alternative ways to increase digital adoption among MSMEs in Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia. Firms were randomly assigned to either (i) a comparison group receiving no support, (ii) a financial subsidy alone, or (iii) the same subsidy paired with technical assistance.

The pilot found that money alone was not the most effective approach. Firms that also received technical assistance used digital tools 2.5 times more intensively than those with the subsidy alone. This is evidence that capability, not just cash, was a constraint to digitalisation.

What does good allocation of public resources look like?

Return on public investment is the benefit attributable to a programme divided by its cost. “Attributable” requires knowing what the programme beneficiaries would have done in the absence of the programme (the counterfactual).

That is especially hard when beneficiaries are not assigned to programmes at random but are selected by the Managing Authority. Beneficiaries may differ from those that were not selected, e.g. they may be more motivated or have more resources. Most of these traits are never recorded in any database or questionnaire. When the beneficiaries later do better on the outcomes of interest than the applicants that were not selected, it is impossible to tell how much of that difference is due to the programme.

Consider an illustrative innovation-grant scheme: EUR 50,000 to each of 100 firms, where “success” means launching a new product. Compare two selection rules.

Strategy A: fund the top performers

Firms that succeed: 80 of 100

Would have succeeded anyway: 65

Succeeded because of the grant: 15

Additionality rate: 19%

Strategy A looks stronger at the first glance as it is associated with 80 successes but only 15 of these succeeded because of the grant.

Strategy B: fund the marginal firm

Firms that succeed: 55 of 100

Would have succeeded anyway: 20

Succeeded because of the grant: 35

Additionality rate: 64%

Strategy B causes 35 successes and thus is more impactful than A.

Raw success counts can be misleading. Programmes need to be judged on impact, not on raw success to evaluate their effectiveness.

Deadweight: low additionality

Additionality is the share of activity that would not have happened without the programme. Top performers would likely have invested anyway, so public funds mostly substitute for money they would have spent regardless - generating little that is truly additional per euro.

Selection bias in measurement

Comparing funded, stronger firms with unfunded, weaker ones credits the programme with firms’ pre-existing quality, not with what the grant did.

Where the grant buys most

Support actually changes behaviour for the marginal, constrained firm: the good project that could not otherwise get funded.

Can experts spot the marginal firm?

Evidence suggests not reliably. In a business plan competition in Nigeria, judges’ scores did not predict which firms would survive or grow three years later (McKenzie & Sansone, 2019; see Common myths).

Finding the counterfactual

As defined above, the counterfactual is what would have happened to programme participants without the programme. It can never be observed directly, so it has to be estimated.

Strategy. Find a group of firms that on average look like treated firms would have looked in the absence of the programme. This group is the comparison group.

There are two ways of estimating the counterfactual:

Ex-ante: Randomised controlled trials

Create the comparison group up front, by randomly assigning who receives the programme. Randomisation is what guarantees the two groups look the same on average.

Ex-post: quasi-experimental designs

Look for a suitable comparison group after the programme has already been rolled out, using natural variation in who received the programme and who did not.

Selection bias is the risk that the comparison group does not actually look like the treatment group on average. If that assumption is wrong, the impact estimate is biased.

North‑East Romania: the counterfactual In North-East Romania, the counterfactual is the group of eligible firms that were not drawn in the lottery. How the lottery created it.

What is a randomised controlled trial?

Intuition. Randomly assign the programme so one group receives it (the treatment group) and one does not (the comparison group). Because the two groups start out the same, the comparison group’s outcome shows what would have happened to the treatment group without the programme.

Because assignment is decided by chance, the two groups are alike on average not only in characteristics you can measure, but also in those you cannot. Take the example of firms applying for a grant: randomising who receives the money means that, on average, the two groups look the same on observable characteristics such as size, sector or turnover, and also on unobservable ones such as management quality or motivation. Nothing should systematically differ between them except that some applicants were lucky in the draw and others were not. That is why no statistical adjustment for firm characteristics is needed to get a credible estimate.

What “random” means here. Not randomly sampling a sub-population to study, but randomly deciding, among qualifying applicants, who receives the programme and who does not.

Advantage. RCTs are considered the gold standard of quantitative impact evaluation because:

  • No assumptions about outcome levels or trends between groups are necessary, as long as randomisation is implemented correctly
  • They deliver strong evidence
  • They are easy to interpret

North‑East Romania: the lottery and its comparison group

Firms first completed an online pre-assessment survey. The 1,038 firms that met the pre-set eligibility criteria all qualified. Because demand exceeded the budget, a public lottery decided which 519 would be invited to apply formally; the other 519 formed the comparison group.

Before any grants were awarded, the two groups were statistically identical:

Not selected Selected
Average DESI score (0-12) 4.30 4.30
Average employees, 2022 (admin data) 13.8 13.8
Share with fewer than 10 employees 63% 63%
Share in urban areas 84% 84%

Because the groups started out the same, the difference between them over time is the impact of being invited to apply for the grant.

The randomisation design

Randomisation can improve legitimacy and trust in programmes

Randomly selecting beneficiaries among eligible applicants is fair. When more applications are eligible than there is budget to fund, a lottery is a transparent way to decide who gets in. It treats every eligible applicant the same rather than relying on favouritism or timing.

Illustration:

North‑East Romania: additional measures to increase trust

North-East Romania’s draw was run in front of a notary: the random number seed drawn, the selection code run, and the resulting balance between groups reviewed. This increased firms’ confidence that selection was fair. The draw also made selection faster. Because beneficiaries were drawn at random from the eligible pool, no ranking of the “best” applications was needed. Evaluators only had to check that each firm met the formal criteria and that the requested funds were justified.

What do you want to learn?

Whether you have sufficient evidence that your programme works, or you are still working out who it reaches and how to design it, there is a case for experimentation. The starting question is whether the programme is working as well as it could be.

Does the programme cause an effect at all?

You do not yet know whether the programme works, or you are still working out who it reaches and how it should be designed before it starts. This requires finding a credible comparison group before the programme launches. Example: in North-East Romania, a lottery decided which eligible firms were invited to apply for a digitalisation grant, and the others formed the comparison group (see above).

How do you deliver it better?

You already know the programme works. The programme’s implementation may already be ongoing. What you want to know is which version of the programme works best. Example: The Digitrans example compares whether a grant alone versus a grant combined with technical assistance performs best.

North‑East Romania: testing outreach In fact, the North-East Romania programme asked both questions. It used an experiment to find out if the programme is effective and a second experiment to study how to best conduct outreach. Before the digitalisation call, the regional development agency randomly selected 26 of 46 towns for in-person information visits, in which a consultant explained the call and helped firms complete the pre-assessment survey. The visited towns produced 110 applications against 71 in the towns that did not receive in-person visits (55% more). The additional applicants in towns with in-person visits were smaller firms (12.6 employees on average, compared with 19.6).

Colombia: group vs individual management support Iacovone et al. 2022 compared two ways to deliver management consulting to auto-parts firms: one-to-one (about US$28,950 per firm) and in groups of 3-8 firms (about US$10,500). Both raised management scores by around 9 percentage points, but only group consulting significantly raised sales and profits.

Choosing the right evaluation design

Within the quasi-experimental branch, here is how the three methods compare:

What if you cannot randomise?

For the question of whether a programme has an effect at all, an RCT only works if randomisation is built in from day one, integrated at design stage before the programme launches (ex-ante), not added afterwards. The comparison group, sample size, and data to collect all depend on those early decisions. When early randomisation for this question is not possible, quasi-experimental methods are the alternative as these are usually conducted after a programme (ex-post). This section provides an overview of the most common quasi-experimental methods.

However, if what you want to know is how best to run a programme that is already launched, rather than whether it works at all, this constraint does not bind in the same way (see above).

Quasi-experimental methods always rely on assumptions, some stronger than others. If those assumptions do not hold, the impact estimate is biased. Many of these assumptions are not testable. The credibility of the evaluation depends on the credibility of the assumptions made.

Pre-post (before and after)
Method
Compare programme participants before and after the programme.
Identifying assumption
In the absence of the programme, participants would have had the same measured outcomes as before. That means no other factors or events, for example economic shocks, affect the outcome besides the programme. This is a strong assumption that rarely holds in practice.

For example, sales might rise after a grant simply because the whole regional economy is recovering at the same time, not because of the programme.

Simple difference
Method
Compare programme participants with non-participants.
Identifying assumption
There is no difference between participants and non-participants besides programme participation. The reason for participation must be unrelated to outcomes. Again, this is unlikely to hold in practice.

For example, firms that apply for a grant are often more ambitious or better managed than those that do not, so they might grow faster regardless of the grant.

Difference-in-differences
Method
Find a group with the same trend and compare differences over time.
Identifying assumption
Any other factors affecting participants over time are the same for non-participants, so both groups would follow the same trajectory in the absence of treatment. This is the parallel trends assumption, and it cannot be tested.

For example, if grant recipients are concentrated in areas hit harder by a regional downturn than the comparison firms, their trends could diverge even without the programme.

Matching
Method
Participants are matched with non-participants who share the same observable characteristics. Matching tries to mimic an RCT. It can be done in different ways: a participant can be matched with one non-participant, or with a weighted average of all similar non-participants, where closer matches count more.
Identifying assumption
There are no differences between participants and matched non-participants in unobserved characteristics that affect the outcome.

For example, two firms matched on size and sector might still differ in the owner’s ambition or management quality, both of which would likely affect outcomes.

Regression discontinuity design
Method
Compare participants who were just eligible with those who were just ineligible. This does not measure impact for all participants, only for those at the cut-off, which is why the result is called a local average treatment effect. The advantage is that unobservable characteristics stop being a concern.
Identifying assumption
This design assumes that
  • the programme is administered following pre-defined cut-off rules
  • the criteria determining who falls above or below are not manipulated
  • a large number of units sit around the cut-off
  • nothing else changes suddenly at the cut-off

For example, if firms know grants are awarded below a certain revenue threshold, some might understate revenue slightly to stay just below it, so firms just below the cut-off are no longer comparable to those just above.

When not to run an RCT

01

Not the right tool

The question is excellent but is not about impact, for example satisfaction with services or cost per service. Monitoring and feedback data are easier and do not need a counterfactual.

02

Not yet

The theory of change is not clear, or implementation is unlikely to work because the markets to serve do not exist.

03

Statistically not feasible

Too few units or too little money. Large indirect or spillover effects you cannot measure. (Spillovers alone are not a reason to drop an RCT: if expected, they can be designed for and measured; see Step 4: check whether an RCT is feasible.) Setting too chaotic, for example disaster relief. Treatment assigned at too high a level, for example national policy.

04

Maybe not worth it for learning

The answer is already known from credible existing studies, or there is no generalisable “why” because the programme is one-off with little scale or replication potential.

If an RCT is the right tool in principle, Step 4: check whether an RCT is feasible checks whether it is feasible in practice: oversubscription, timing, legal basis, institutional buy-in and measurable outcomes.

Learning through RCTs is a continuous process

Every RCT raises new questions along with the ones it answers, each round building on the last. That is how we expand our knowledge of what works, for whom, and under what conditions, and use it to design better programmes. Exchange within a community of practice matters, so we can learn from each other, not just from our own results.

But before adopting what worked elsewhere, questions of external validity need to be considered, as what worked in one place may not necessarily work in another. One needs to ask: are the conditions comparable, i.e. is the target group similar and are the conditions essential to that result actually in place in my setting?

Review Before considering how to embed an RCT, review a programme’s theory of change, identify risks and the learning questions this round should answer, and the outcomes most likely to change. Review lessons from other settings and countries and adapt them. Identify baseline needs of potential beneficiaries in your region.

Design Choose a credible comparison group and set the sample size needed to detect a meaningful effect. Turn the outcomes identified in Review into measurable indicators using primary and secondary data, and set out the data collection plan, budget, and timeline in a Terms of Reference.

Test Test different programme designs and delivery modes to identify what works best. Assess for whom the programme works best. Address take-up challenges. Learn about mechanisms, unintended effects, and crowding out.

Invest Get a quantitative and credible impact estimate that can guide future investments. Conduct cost-benefit analyses to avoid wasting money and to inform programme decisions. Scale up based on evidence.
