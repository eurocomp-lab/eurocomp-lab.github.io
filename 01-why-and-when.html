Why and when to use a randomised controlled trial

Summary

What is the real impact of an intervention?

Definition. Impact is the difference between what happens after an intervention is introduced and what would have happened in its absence. What would have happened in its absence is called the counterfactual.

The dilemma. The counterfactual is a “what if” and cannot be observed. Ideally, the same participant would be observed twice, once with the intervention and once without it, and the two compared. That is impossible: each participant either takes part or does not. Every impact evaluation method is a way of approximating that missing second observation.

The solution. Impact evaluation methods differ in how they estimate the counterfactual. This page provides an overview of the main approaches and the role of RCTs among them.

What does impact evaluation measure exactly?

Monitoring tracks what an intervention delivers and how the situation of the target group develops, using output and result indicators. Impact evaluation goes one step further: it estimates how much of the change in results was caused by the intervention, by comparing what happened with the counterfactual.

Outputs

What an intervention delivers. For example, grants disbursed, participants enrolled, training sessions run.

Results

What changes for participants or the target group. For example, employment, skills, energy savings, firm growth.

Before implementation, the activities of an intervention can be mapped against the change they are expected to bring about. This mapping shows, in advance, why each step should lead to the next. That causal logic – the how and why behind each step – explains the intervention’s reason for existing. The mapping also shows what needs to be measured to test whether the expected change is actually happening.

But which results should be looked at exactly? An intervention can change many things, and not all of them can or should be measured. To know which results to look at, and when to expect them to change, the starting point is to spell out how the intervention is supposed to work: how its inputs and activities lead to outputs, and how those outputs are expected to lead to results.

This chain is the foundation of a theory of change, and having thought it through helps in designing a strong impact evaluation. There is more on this in Step 1: develop a Theory of Change.

Making the chain explicit also surfaces the assumptions behind each link, and shows which of them the intervention’s impact depends on most. If one of these assumptions does not hold (for example, that firms will take up the support they are offered), the intervention may fall short of its intended impact. Such weak links can be tested directly ( see below under How can it be delivered better?).

Challenges in measuring impact

The value of an intervention lies in the change it causes: its additionality. Results alone do not show it, because some participants would have achieved them anyway.

Consider an illustrative innovation-grant scheme: EUR 50,000 to each of 100 firms, where “success” means launching a new product. Compare two selection rules.

Raw success counts can be misleading: to assess effectiveness, interventions need to be judged on their impact. Which applicants to target remains a policy choice, set by the intervention’s objectives. Cohesion policy may deliberately support those whose direct returns are lower, to reduce disparities.

In practice, nobody knows how many participants would have succeeded anyway: the 65 and the 20 in the example are counterfactual numbers. The obvious workaround is to compare participants with applicants who were not selected. That runs into a second problem.

Selected applicants may differ from those who were not selected; for example, they may be more motivated or have more resources. Under Strategy A, funded firms would outperform unfunded applicants even if the grant did nothing. Most of these traits are never recorded in any database or questionnaire, so it is impossible to tell how much of the difference is due to the intervention.

Selection bias arises when the comparison group differs from the participants in ways that also affect results. In that case, the impact estimate is biased.

Measuring additionality therefore requires a comparison group that differs from participants only in not receiving the intervention. Part 2 shows how to form one. ↑ Back to the overview

Finding the counterfactual

The counterfactual can never be observed directly, so it has to be estimated.

Strategy. Find a group that, on average, looks like the participants would have looked without the intervention. This group is the comparison group.

Comparison groups can be formed in two ways, depending on how participants are assigned to the intervention:

Random assignment: randomised controlled trials

The comparison group is created before the intervention starts, by randomly assigning who receives it. Random assignment makes the two groups similar on average and so avoids the selection bias described above.

Non-random assignment: quasi-experimental methods

Participants are selected in another way, for example through applications, scores or eligibility rules, and the comparison group is drawn from those who did not take part. It can be identified before the intervention starts (for example, with baseline data and a known selection rule) or after it has been rolled out.

How an RCT creates a comparison group

Intuition. As explained on the home page, the intervention is assigned at random, so the treatment group and the comparison group are similar on average at the start. The comparison group’s results therefore show what would have happened to the treatment group without the intervention.

Because assignment is decided by chance, the two groups are similar on average not only in characteristics that can be measured but also in those that cannot. Take applicants for support, whether firms applying for a grant or jobseekers applying for a training course: randomising who receives it means that, on average, the two groups are similar in observable characteristics – such as size and sector for firms, or age and education for jobseekers – and also in unobservable ones, such as management quality or motivation. Apart from the luck of the draw, nothing should systematically differ between them. That is why, in a well-implemented RCT, no statistical adjustment for applicant characteristics is needed to obtain a credible estimate.

What “random” means here. Not randomly sampling a sub-population to study, but randomly deciding, among eligible applicants, who receives the intervention and who does not (or who receives another intervention).

Strengths and requirements. The Commission’s Better Regulation toolbox (Tool #68) describes randomised controlled trials as “the benchmark for scientific quantitative methods”. Their main strengths are:

  • They provide strong causal evidence: because assignment is random, a difference in results between the groups can be attributed to the intervention rather than to pre-existing differences
  • No assumptions about levels or trends between groups are needed, as long as randomisation is implemented correctly
  • The estimate is simple to calculate and to explain: it is the difference in average results between the groups

RCTs also have requirements. Random assignment has to be built into the selection process, usually before the intervention starts. Enough eligible participants are needed to detect an effect, and the assignment has to be respected during implementation. When an RCT may not be the right choice below discusses these further.

Randomisation can support transparency and trust

When more applications are eligible than the budget can fund, a lottery among eligible applicants is a transparent way to decide who receives support. It gives every eligible applicant the same chance, and the selection rule can be explained and verified in advance.

Illustration:

North‑East Romania: additional measures to increase trust

North-East Romania’s draw was run in front of a notary: the random number seed drawn, the selection code run, and the resulting balance between groups reviewed. This increased firms’ confidence that selection was fair. The draw also made selection faster. Because beneficiaries were drawn at random from the eligible pool, no ranking of the “best” applications was needed. Evaluators only had to check that each firm met the formal criteria and that the requested funds were justified.

Quasi-experimental methods

When participants are not assigned at random, quasi-experimental methods estimate the counterfactual by comparing participants with non-participants under specific assumptions. They can be planned before an intervention starts – for example, around an existing scoring rule – or applied once it has been rolled out, using data that already exist. The overview below is not exhaustive: it presents some of the most common methods. The Commission’s Better Regulation toolbox (Tool #68) describes further ones, such as synthetic controls.

Quasi-experimental methods always rely on assumptions, some stronger than others. If those assumptions do not hold, the impact estimate is biased. Many of these assumptions are not testable directly, so they need to be stated and discussed. The credibility of the evaluation depends on the credibility of the assumptions made.

Pre-post (before and after)
Method
Compare participants before and after the intervention.
Identifying assumption
In the absence of the intervention, participants would have had the same measured results as before. That means no other factors or events, for example economic shocks, affect the result besides the intervention. This is a strong assumption that rarely holds in practice.

For example, sales might rise after a grant simply because the whole regional economy is recovering at the same time, not because of the intervention.

Simple difference
Method
Compare participants with non-participants.
Identifying assumption
There is no difference between participants and non-participants besides participation in the intervention. The reason for participation must be unrelated to results. Again, this is unlikely to hold in practice.

For example, firms that apply for a grant are often more ambitious or better managed than those that do not, so they might grow faster regardless of the grant.

Difference-in-differences
Method
Find a group with the same trend and compare differences over time.
Identifying assumption
Any other factors affecting participants over time are the same for non-participants, so both groups would follow the same trajectory in the absence of treatment. This is the parallel trends assumption, and it cannot be tested.

For example, if grant recipients are concentrated in areas hit harder by a regional downturn than the comparison firms, their trends could diverge even without the intervention.

Matching
Method
Participants are matched with non-participants who share the same observable characteristics. Matching tries to mimic an RCT. It can be done in different ways: a participant can be matched with one non-participant, or with a weighted average of all similar non-participants, where closer matches count more.
Identifying assumption
There are no differences between participants and matched non-participants in unobserved characteristics that affect the result.

For example, two firms matched on size and sector might still differ in the owner’s ambition or management quality, both of which would likely affect results.

Regression discontinuity design
Method
Compare participants who were just eligible with those who were just ineligible. This does not measure impact for all participants, only for those at the cut-off, which is why the estimate is called a local average treatment effect. The advantage is that unobservable characteristics stop being a concern.
Identifying assumption
This design assumes that
  • the intervention is administered following pre-defined cut-off rules
  • the criteria determining who falls above or below are not manipulated
  • a large number of units sit around the cut-off
  • nothing else changes suddenly at the cut-off

For example, if firms know grants are awarded below a certain revenue threshold, some might understate revenue slightly to stay just below it, so firms just below the cut-off are no longer comparable to those just above.

Instrumental variables
Method
Use a variable – the instrument – that influences whether units take part in the intervention but is otherwise unrelated to the results of interest. The difference in results between units with different values of the instrument, divided by the difference in their participation rates, gives the effect of taking part for those whose participation the instrument changed. The Better Regulation toolbox (Tool #68) illustrates the approach with the Covenant of Mayors in Italy: the presence of a territorial coordinator offering technical support made municipalities more likely to join, and was assumed to be otherwise unrelated to the mayors’ re-election results (Martelli et al., 2018). A randomly assigned encouragement, such as an invitation or an information visit, can also serve as an instrument; the same logic is used in RCTs where not everyone offered support takes it up.
Identifying assumption
The instrument must clearly influence participation, and it must affect results only through participation. This requirement is commonly called the exclusion restriction. This condition cannot be tested and has to be argued. The estimate applies only to those whose participation was changed by the instrument, not to all participants, and data on the instrument are needed for both participants and non-participants.

For example, a local business adviser who promotes a grant may make firms more likely to apply. But if the same adviser also gives firms other help, the adviser’s presence affects results through more than the grant, and the exclusion restriction does not hold.

What can be learned from an RCT?

Whether or not there is already good evidence that an intervention works, there can be a case for experimentation. The starting question is whether the intervention is working as well as it could be.

Does the intervention have an effect at all?

It is not yet known whether the intervention works, or who it reaches and how it should be designed. Answering this requires a credible comparison group, which for an RCT means randomising before the intervention launches. Example: in North-East Romania, a lottery decided which eligible firms were invited to apply for a digitalisation grant, and the others formed the comparison group (see above).

How can it be delivered better?

There is already evidence that the intervention works, and implementation may already be under way. The question is which version of the intervention works best. Example: the Digitrans pilot below compares a grant alone with a grant combined with technical assistance.

Comparing versions is also a way to address weak links in the theory of change. Where impact depends on an assumption that may not hold, for example that firms will take up the support they are offered, alternative ways of addressing it can be tested within the same evaluation.

Digitrans example The Digitrans pilot (see Examples for more details) tested and compared two alternative ways to increase digital adoption among MSMEs in Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia. Firms were randomly assigned to either (i) a comparison group receiving no support, (ii) a financial subsidy alone, or (iii) the same subsidy paired with technical assistance.

The pilot found that money alone was not the most effective approach. Firms that also received technical assistance used digital tools 2.5 times more intensively than those with the subsidy alone. This is evidence that capability, not just cash, was a constraint to digitalisation.

North‑East Romania: testing outreach The North-East Romania intervention asked both questions. It used one experiment to find out whether the grants were effective and a second to study how best to conduct outreach. Before the digitalisation call, the regional development agency randomly selected 26 of 46 towns for in-person information visits, in which a consultant explained the call and helped firms complete the pre-assessment survey. The 26 visited towns produced 110 applications against 71 in the 20 towns without visits, about 4.2 per town against 3.6. Applicants from visited towns were also smaller firms (12.6 employees on average, compared with 19.6).

Colombia: group vs individual management support Iacovone et al. 2022 compared two ways to deliver management consulting to auto-parts firms: one-to-one (about USD 28,950 per firm) and in groups of 3–8 firms (about USD 10,500). Both raised management scores by around 9 percentage points, but only group consulting significantly raised sales and profits.

Choosing an evaluation design

As this page discussed, RCTs are one of several ways to estimate the counterfactual, distinguished by participants being assigned at random (Finding the counterfactual). Whether an RCT is the most suitable approach, or whether another works better for the intervention at hand, depends on what the evaluation should find out, when it can be planned and whether there are more eligible applicants than places, so that participation can be randomised.

The decision tree below gives an overview of how these factors point towards an RCT or a quasi-experimental method. It is a simplified guide. In practice, other considerations can make a quasi-experimental design the better choice even when an RCT would be possible – for example, when legal, political or ethical considerations limit random selection or when all eligible applicants need to be served at the same time. The budget and the wider context of the intervention also play a part.

When an RCT may not be the right choice

01

Not the right tool

The question is excellent but is not about impact, for example satisfaction with services or cost per service. Monitoring and feedback data are easier and do not need a counterfactual.

02

Not yet

The theory of change is not clear, or implementation is unlikely to work because key conditions, such as functioning markets or delivery capacity, are not yet in place.

03

Statistically not feasible

Too few units or too little money. Large indirect or spillover effects that cannot be measured. (Spillovers alone are not a reason to drop an RCT: if expected, they can be designed for and measured; see Step 5: how to randomise.) Support that has to reach all eligible recipients at once, for example emergency aid after a natural disaster. Treatment assigned at too high a level, for example regional policy.

04

Maybe not worth it for learning

The answer is already known from credible existing studies, or there is no generalisable “why” because the intervention is one-off with little scale or replication potential.

If an RCT is the right tool in principle, Step 4: check whether an RCT is feasible checks whether it is feasible in practice: oversubscription, timing, legal basis, institutional buy-in and measurable results.

Learning through RCTs is a continuous process

Every RCT raises new questions along with the ones it answers, each round building on the last. That is how knowledge of what works, for whom and under what conditions grows, and how it feeds into better interventions. Exchange within a community of practice matters, so that managing authorities learn from each other, not just from their own findings.

But before adopting what worked elsewhere, questions of external validity need to be considered, as what worked in one place may not necessarily work in another. The key question is whether the conditions are comparable: is the target group similar, and are the conditions essential to that effect actually in place in the new setting?

Review Before considering how to embed an RCT, review the intervention’s theory of change, identify risks and the learning questions this round should answer, and the results most likely to change. Review lessons from other settings and countries and adapt them. Identify baseline needs of potential beneficiaries in the region.

Design Choose a credible comparison group and set the sample size needed to detect a meaningful effect. Turn the results identified in Review into measurable indicators using primary and secondary data, and set out the data collection plan, budget and timeline in terms of reference.

Test Test different intervention designs and delivery modes to identify what works best. Assess for whom the intervention works best. Address take-up challenges. Learn about mechanisms, unintended effects and crowding out.

Invest Get a quantitative and credible impact estimate that can guide future investments. Conduct cost-benefit analyses to avoid wasting money and to inform funding decisions. Scale up based on evidence.

Common questions about RCTs

Are RCTs only for academics?

No. The basic idea is simple: who receives the intervention is decided by chance, like a coin toss, so the treatment and comparison groups are similar on average. The impact estimate is then the difference in average results between the two groups, which is straightforward to explain to non-specialists.

Designing and running an RCT does require careful planning and some technical support, for example for sample size calculations and for running the random draw – as do other rigorous evaluation methods, which in turn need their assumptions checked. With that support in place, RCTs are transparent and relatively easy to communicate.

Is random assignment allowed under EU rules, and does it contradict selection on merit?

Random assignment is allowed and can be a fair way to select among equally eligible applicants. The Directorate-General for Regional and Urban Policy (DG REGIO) has formally confirmed that randomisation is permissible under EU cohesion policy rules (for more information, see A legal basis for random selection).

Random assignment does not replace selection on merit but can supplement it. To illustrate this, imagine a two-stage process in which all applicants are first assessed against the call’s eligibility and quality criteria as usual. In the second stage, only those who pass enter the draw. Expert panels still decide who is eligible, but they do not rank or choose among equally eligible applicants. Interventions can also be designed to fund the strongest applicants outright and randomise only within a middle band of scores, as was done in Extremadura.

Randomisation is already used to select beneficiaries across Europe. Managing authorities and agencies in Spain, Czechia, Croatia and Romania have used random assignment to allocate EU-funded support, as has the European Commission itself for SME energy-efficiency grants across 34 countries, and interventions in Bulgaria and Poland are preparing to do the same (see Examples).

Why are RCTs often called the gold standard?

Because they provide robust causal evidence. Random assignment makes the treatment and comparison groups similar on average, in characteristics that can be measured as well as in those that cannot, so a difference in results between the groups can be attributed to the intervention rather than to pre-existing differences. No assumptions about how the groups would otherwise have evolved, such as parallel trends, are needed, as long as randomisation is implemented correctly. And the estimate is simple to calculate and to explain: it is the difference in average results between the groups. The Commission’s Better Regulation toolbox (Tool #68) accordingly describes randomised controlled trials as “the benchmark for scientific quantitative methods”.

This does not make an RCT the right choice in every case. It has to be feasible, ethical and well implemented, and some questions are better answered with other methods.

Are RCTs more expensive than other rigorous methods?

Randomisation is an allocation rule. It determines who is assigned to an intervention and who is not, but says nothing about how results are measured afterwards. Measuring results (for example, employment, energy savings or firm turnover) requires data, which costs money and takes time – not just for RCTs but for whichever evaluation method is used. RCTs usually plan a baseline survey from the design stage; with a large enough sample they do not strictly need one, though collecting one is good practice. Where results are already recorded in administrative registers, the cost of an RCT is similar to that of any other rigorous evaluation: legal and GDPR clearance to access and link the registers, and analytical time.

RCTs do come with additional staffing costs. Unlike a method applied retrospectively to data that already exists, randomisation must be run and monitored while the intervention is ongoing, so the managing authority needs staff assigned to it on an ongoing basis.

Can RCTs show why an intervention works?

Only to the extent that the design allows. A standard RCT shows whether an intervention made a difference, not through which channels. Specific pathways can be tested with a multi-arm design: participants are randomised across several treatment arms that receive different components or combinations of components (for example, a grant alone versus a grant combined with business advisory support), and results are compared across arms. Data on intermediate results and qualitative methods, such as interviews with participants, can help explain the mechanisms behind an estimated effect.

What exactly does an RCT measure?

The effect of whatever was randomised – most commonly, being assigned to (or offered) an intervention. That is why it matters to get the learning questions right at the design stage. To separate the effect of one component from another, those components need to be randomised separately too, for example through a multi-arm design.

Do RCT findings apply beyond the study population?

Every empirical method faces the question of whether findings hold beyond the sample they were estimated on, so external validity is not a concern unique to randomisation. A single RCT cannot show whether an intervention will work everywhere, but it can show a lot about the conditions under which it worked in that case. When a similar intervention is designed elsewhere, it is possible to assess how similar those conditions are and judge how far the original findings are likely to apply. The more similar RCTs are run across different regions and countries, the more confidently general conclusions can be drawn.

Can RCTs deliver findings within a programming period?

A staggered or phased roll-out combined with an RCT can be a source of continuous learning. Early waves of participants already generate short-term results while later waves are still being enrolled, so a managing authority can see early signals – take-up, engagement, first movements in results – well before the intervention is fully rolled out, and adjust design or targeting for later waves accordingly. Long-term results can, by construction, only be observed after a few years, which may come too late to change the current programming period. But similar interventions are likely to follow, in the same region or elsewhere in the EU, so knowing whether an intervention achieved its intended effect still helps allocate future funding more efficiently.

Do RCTs assume everyone responds the same way?

No. The effects an RCT most commonly measures – the average treatment effect or the intention-to-treat effect – compare average results between the treatment and comparison groups. Neither assumes that every member of these groups responds identically, only that the average difference is a meaningful summary of the intervention’s impact. With a large enough sample, effects can also be analysed by subgroup. Administrative registers often already record the characteristics on which effects may differ (for example, size, sector or region), which makes it relatively cheap to check whether an intervention works differently for different groups.

Is further analysis needed after randomisation?

Yes. If sample sizes are large enough and treatment is assigned randomly, the treatment and comparison groups will be similar on average – not that every participant has an identical match in the other group, only that characteristics balance on average. Good practice is to check this balance on observable characteristics at baseline, before the intervention starts. In practice, participants also do not always stay in the group they were originally assigned to. This happens when randomisation is on assignment to an intervention rather than on take-up: the assigned and non-assigned groups remain comparable, but those who actually participate may differ from those who do not. In this case, what is reported is the intention-to-treat effect – the average effect of being assigned to the intervention, rather than of participating in it.

How large does the sample need to be?

Detecting a given effect with confidence needs enough observations in each group – the smaller the expected effect, the larger the sample must be. This is why power calculations are carried out at the design stage.

A power calculation needs a few inputs: the smallest effect that would still be meaningful to detect, how much the result already varies across participants before the intervention runs, expected take-up of the intervention (as this affects the effective size of the treatment group), how much loss of observations to expect over time and whether assignment happens at the level of the individual applicant or of a group they belong to. Assigning by group generally needs a larger total sample to reach the same precision.

See Power calculations, part of Step 4, for a walkthrough of turning these inputs into an actual sample size calculation.

Is it difficult to get responses from the comparison group?

It can be, so it is worth planning for at the design stage. Often, though, results of interest are already captured in administrative registers rather than collected through a survey, so comparison group response rates are not a concern.

If data on results need to be collected through surveys, a group that received nothing may feel less invested in answering than a group that participated. Looking across dozens of studies, World Bank economist David McKenzie found that differential attrition between treatment and comparison groups averages only a few percentage points. The gap widens mainly when the treatment is large and highly visible, such as a sizeable cash grant. Examples on this site show both cases. In Extremadura, there was no evidence of selective attrition in follow-up survey responses. In the European Commission’s SME energy-efficiency grants, firms offered a grant of up to EUR 10,000 answered follow-up surveys far more often than the comparison group (91% against 65%), so the estimates rely on statistical corrections for who responded. Sustained follow-up – repeat calls, alternative contact channels, incentives for non-respondents – can help close such gaps, as in Czechia.

Is it unethical to have a comparison group?

Not necessarily. Where the budget does not allow all eligible applicants to be funded, some are turned away whatever the evaluation method; randomisation only determines how the available funds are allocated, giving every equally eligible applicant the same chance. Where funds would allow a wider roll-out, starting with part of the eligible population can still be justified if the aim is to learn whether the intervention works before scaling it up – for example through a phased roll-out in which the comparison group receives support later. Other ways of choosing among equally eligible applicants are not necessarily more accurate: in a business plan competition in Nigeria, expert judges’ scores had no relationship with which businesses went on to survive, hire staff or turn a profit three years later (McKenzie and Sansone, 2019).

Nor does a comparison group have to mean receiving nothing at all: RCTs can compare two different versions of an intervention, so every participant receives some form of support and the question becomes which version works better. What matters is that participants are properly informed about the study, their rights and how any data on them are processed and stored – which is why RCTs are often reviewed beforehand by a research ethics committee (an Institutional Review Board, or IRB, in some countries), which is also the body participants can turn to with complaints.

Do RCTs require ethical clearance?

Not always. Whether ethics review is legally mandatory depends on the country, the institution running the study, the type of intervention and whether personal data are involved. For example, an RCT studying an intervention’s effect on firms using administrative data to measure results often does not require ethical clearance. Seeking it anyway is still recommended: a research ethics committee helps ensure that participants are properly informed, that data handling complies with GDPR and that there is a body participants can turn to with complaints.
