Examples of RCTs in public programmes

Summary

This page presents examples of public programmes that built experimentation into their design. For each one, it describes the problem the programme set out to address, how the programme and its evaluation were designed, and what it achieved or is expected to show. The aim is to give readers a sense of which kinds of experiments are feasible, and under what conditions they work well. Most examples come from the EuroCOMP Lab and its precursors. Others come from researchers and institutions outside the Lab.

Spain: financial support to innovate

Extremadura, SpainBin-ranking RCT56 firms in the middle band2024–2025

The problem

Market failures keep research and development (R&D) investment in Extremadura below its optimal level. Small and medium-sized enterprises (SMEs) in the region under-invest in innovation, particularly in agriculture and the ecological transition.

What was done

The Regional Government of Extremadura (JUNTA) and the Extremadura Science and Technology Park Foundation (FUNDECYT-PCTEx) created an EU-funded matching grant programme for small and medium firms.

Each cycle awards EUR 3.5 million in grants, up to EUR 250,000 per firm (EUR 138,000 on average), financing 35% to 80% of a firm’s proposed R&D investment.

What experimentation sought to answer

“What is the impact of R&D grants on SMEs’ business performance?”

The programme

The programme
Matching grants of up to EUR 250,000 per firm (average EUR 138,000) for R&D projects
Where
Extremadura, Spain; regional government (JUNTA) with FUNDECYT-PCTEx, EU-funded
Who is randomised
Firms: SME applicants, mainly agriculture and ecological transition, scored on 11 criteria
The design
Bin-ranking RCT: the middle bin randomised, 22 funded vs. 34 not funded
Outcomes tracked
Private R&D investment, implementation success, expected sales growth
Starting point
Market failures keep regional R&D investment below its optimal level

Key steps and dates

→

→

Follow-up surveyMar–Apr 2025

How the programme was tested

Extremadura ran an advertising campaign to attract at least 100 applicants. External reviewers scored every application on 11 objective criteria and grouped proposals into low, medium and high quality.

Why this design. Strength: it leverages investment-return uncertainty for applications with unclear returns. Limitation: it requires a large sample size in the middle range.

Randomisation successfully balanced key firm characteristics: the comparison group and the treatment group looked the same on average. There was no evidence of firms with certain characteristics dropping out of the study more than others (selective attrition) in follow-up survey responses.

What the programme achieved

Three months after receiving the grant, firms in the treatment group reported higher planned R&D investment and stronger confidence in their projects than the comparison group.

+48%

More planned R&D investment for 2025

EUR 65,000 above the comparison-group average.

67%

Probability of successful implementation

Up from 16% without funding, according to expert priors.

×2

Expected sales growth, conditional on success

From 8% to 16% after receiving the grant, according to expert priors.

Over half of the comparison-group firms planned to continue their R&D projects using other funding or their own resources. Public R&D subsidies can significantly boost private investment, but careful targeting is needed to avoid simply displacing money firms would have spent anyway.

Czechia: light-touch support for SMEs

Moravian-Silesian, Ústí and Karlovy Vary regionsTwo lotteries419 firms in two pilots2024–2025

The problem

Entrepreneurial activity remains low in Czech regions hit by the decline of the coal industry. These regions need to diversify away from carbon-intensive industries and grow their private sector.

In September 2022, the European Commission approved the Just Transition Programme (JTP): EUR 1.641 billion for business R&D, post-mining regeneration, skills, clean energy and the circular economy in the Moravian-Silesian, Ústí and Karlovy Vary regions.

A key part of the JTP is a voucher scheme for entrepreneurs: up to EUR 21,100 for business development, up to EUR 42,200 for innovation, and up to EUR 21,100 for digital transformation.

What was done

The World Bank tested two light-touch, complementary pilots that add skills support on top of the voucher scheme.

Smart Start (Moravian-Silesian region): seven workshops teaching a scientific approach to decision-making under uncertainty, covering risk versus uncertainty, hypothesis testing and A/B testing, aimed at voucher recipients and other interested firms.

AI Klub (Ústí and Karlovy Vary regions): five hands-on workshops on using AI for efficiency, marketing, sales, product development and financial management, with peer-to-peer exchange between firms.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Does light-touch support to SMEs improve firm capabilities and performance?”

How the two pilots were tested

Both pilots drew their sample from firms that had already shown interest, then invited roughly two in three of them by random selection, leaving the rest as a comparison group.

Croatia: matching businesses with scientists

Croatia, national programmeSimple lottery210 firms2023–2025

The problem

Firms and scientists in Croatia struggle to collaborate: information frictions, uncertain transaction costs and limited outreach budgets get in the way, especially for small and medium firms.

Only 8% of Croatian firms collaborate with higher education institutions, far below the EU average. A World Bank survey found that 47% of firms lacked information about relevant scientists.

What was done

The World Bank, the Innovation Centre Nikola Tesla and the Ministry of Science, Education and Youth designed “Scinergy”, a business-science matchmaking pilot targeting fields such as AI, energy and biotechnology.

Matched firms and scientists took part in a two-day matchmaking event in February 2024: “speed-dating” sessions followed by joint workshops, plus legal and communication support to help pairs plan R&D projects together.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Does connecting firms with scientists foster research partnerships and innovation?”

The programme

The programme
Scinergy: matchmaking events, workshops, and ongoing support for firm-scientist pairs
Where
Croatia; Innovation Centre Nikola Tesla with the Ministry of Science, Education and Youth
Who is randomised
210 eligible firms, from over 250 applications, seeking scientific collaboration
The design
Simple lottery: 100 firms invited to Scinergy vs. 110 not invited
Outcomes tracked
Collaboration attempts and partnerships, innovation, R&D employment
Starting point
Only 8% of Croatian firms collaborate with higher education, far below the EU average

Key steps and dates

→
LotteryFeb 2024
→
Matchmaking eventFeb 2024
→
Joint R&D planningMar–Apr 2024
→
Follow-up surveySpring 2025

How the programme was tested

Firms applied between November 2023 and January 2024. In February 2024, eligible firms were randomly assigned by lottery to receive matchmaking support or not.

Why this design. Strength: a lottery among applicants ensures wide outreach, thanks to a simple application process. Limitation: firms may drop out after applying, which can reduce statistical power.

What the programme achieved

+38%

Collaboration attempts with scientists

Treated firms were more likely to attempt and to sustain collaborations with Croatian researchers.

+50%

Commercialisation of new products

Among firms that originally requested support in AI, who benefited the most from the pilot.

Romania (North-East): financial support to digitalise

North-East region, RomaniaLottery with encouragement to apply1,038 firms2023–2025

The problem

Romanian SMEs lag behind the EU average in digital performance. Key barriers to digital transformation include a lack of financing, awareness, and capacity to capitalise on digital investments.

Among the 1,038 likely eligible firms in North-East Romania, firms met 4.3 of 12 digital indicators on average, and 67% named lack of finance as their main obstacle.

What was done

The North-East Regional Development Agency (RDA) offers non-refundable grants of EUR 15,000 to 100,000 for digital tools, equipment and services, covering 90% of eligible project costs. The first call had EUR 31 million: EUR 26.35 million from the European Regional Development Fund and EUR 4.65 million from the state budget.

The RDA and the World Bank formed a learning partnership, announced publicly at the programme’s launch. The first call was non-competitive and framed as a pilot to generate learning.

What experimentation sought to answer

“What is the impact of digitalisation support on Romanian SMEs’ performance?”

The programme

The programme
Non-refundable grants of EUR 15,000–100,000 for digital tools, equipment and services, covering 90% of eligible costs
Where
North-East Romania; North-East Regional Development Agency (RDA), co-funded by EU Cohesion Policy
Who is randomised
1,038 likely eligible SMEs, identified by a pre-assessment survey from over 1,500 applicants
The design
Lottery among pre-identified applicants, with encouragement to apply: 519 selected and invited to apply vs. 519 not selected
Outcomes tracked
Digital investments, use of digital tools, firm performance
Starting point
Firms met 4.3 of 12 digital indicators on average; 67% named lack of finance as their main obstacle

Key steps and dates

Pre-assessment surveyMay–Sep 2023
→
Lottery before a notaryOct 2023
→
Full proposalsMostly by end of winter 2024
→
Contracts signedMar 2024–Mar 2025
→
Follow-up survey

How the programme was tested

Firms first completed a pre-assessment survey that assessed their digital maturity, recorded their interest in applying and gave them an automated, simplified check of their eligibility. Piloted in May 2023 and closed in September 2023, it identified 1,038 likely eligible SMEs from over 1,500 applicants. In October 2023, 519 of them were selected at random, in front of a notary, and invited to submit full proposals; the other 519 formed the comparison group.

Why this design. Strength: it requires, and so ensures, wide outreach, thanks to a simple application process. Limitation: firms may drop out after they apply, which affects statistical power.

What the programme achieved

The short-term results compare the firms selected in the lottery with the comparison group, whether or not they went on to sign a contract, only a few months after contracts were signed. They are therefore conservative estimates, based on firms’ self-reported survey data.

+101%

Investment in digital tools

Selected firms invested about twice as much in digital tools as the comparison group.

+291%

Spending on digital tools for business administration

Software spending rose by 167% and production-related digital investment by 352%.

+5.6 pp

Firms using ERP software

Against 26% in the comparison group. The share using CRM software was 3.1 percentage points higher than the comparison group’s 11.7%.

No significant short-term effect on profits or sales has been observed yet. Selected firms showed slightly higher sales and labour productivity, but the differences were not statistically significant, likely because implementation was at an early stage. Tailored support and continued monitoring will be essential to ensure inclusive and sustained digital transformation.

The programme also tested outreach. Before the call, the agency randomly selected 26 of 46 towns for in-person information visits. The visited towns produced more applications, and the additional applicants were smaller firms (see What do you want to learn?).

Europe: grants for SME energy efficiency

34 European countriesSimple lottery1,964 SMEs2024–2026

The problem

Energy efficiency is one of the cheapest ways for a firm to cut energy use and costs, yet most European SMEs invest little in it. Among the firms that applied to this programme, only 22% had invested in energy efficiency in the year before applying, even though 62% already monitored their energy use and almost all planned some form of capital investment. Advice on its own was not turning plans into purchases.

What was done

The European Commission (DG GROW and EISMEA), the Enterprise Europe Network (EEN) and the World Bank ran the EENergy pilot, funded by the EU Single Market Programme.

On top of the advisory support EEN advisors already provide, the pilot offered a reimbursement grant of up to EUR 10,000 for energy-efficiency measures: equipment and installations, consultancy, or staff training. The evaluation was designed into the programme from the start, before the first call opened.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Does money on top of advice change what small firms invest in, and does it cut their energy use?”

The programme

Timeline
Call 1 opened February 2024 (1,406 firms); lottery June 2024; activities from July 2024; Call 2 December 2024 to February 2025 (558 firms); follow-up surveys Q1 2025 and Q1 2026; findings September 2026
The programme
EENergy: a reimbursement grant of up to EUR 10,000 for equipment, consultancy or training, on top of standard EEN advisory services
Where
34 European countries; European Commission (DG GROW, EISMEA) and Enterprise Europe Network with the World Bank, funded by the EU Single Market Programme
Who is randomised
1,964 eligible SMEs across two open calls: 54% micro, 36% small, 9% medium-sized; 36% in manufacturing
The design
Simple lottery among eligible applicants, run separately for each call and stratified by consortium and advisor: 986 offered the grant vs. 978 receiving advisory services only
Outcomes tracked
Energy-efficiency and renewable investment, energy use and spending, energy management practices, revenue, profits and employment
Starting point
Only 22% of applicant firms had invested in energy efficiency in the year before the programme, although 62% already monitored their energy use

Key steps and dates

Two open calls
→
Eligibility check
→
Lottery
→
Grants and advisory services
→
Follow-up surveys

How the programme was tested

Firms applied in two open calls, from February to May 2024 and from December 2024 to February 2025. Applications were screened against the EU definition of an SME and against programme requirements, including an energy-efficiency action plan agreed with an EEN advisor and a target of at least 5% lower energy consumption. Eligible firms then entered a lottery, run separately for each call and stratified by consortium and advisor characteristics, so that funded and unfunded firms came from the same pool and worked with comparable advisors.

Why this design. Strength: because the grant was drawn by lottery among firms that all received the same advisory service, the comparison separates the effect of the cash from the effect of the advice, across 34 countries at once. Limitation: firms offered the grant answered follow-up surveys far more often than the standard group (91% against 65%), so the estimates depend on statistical corrections for who responded.

What the programme achieved

One year on, the grant had changed what firms bought and how they managed energy. Total energy-related investment was about EUR 9,700 higher per firm in the grant group, almost all of it capital spending, with renewable-energy installations the single largest category.

+26 pp

Firms investing in energy efficiency

43% of firms offered the grant made an energy-efficiency investment, against 17% of the standard group.

×3

Firms investing in renewables

The share investing in renewable energy rose from 10% to about 30%.

+31 pp

Firms setting energy-reduction goals

83% of the grant group set energy-reduction goals, against 52% of the standard group, alongside more regular energy monitoring.

The response was strongest among the smallest firms. Renewable-energy investment rose by 22 percentage points among micro and small firms, against 5 points among medium-sized firms. The fixed EUR 10,000 was worth roughly 5% of annual revenue for the median micro firm, but only 0.1% for the median medium-sized firm, which is the clearest explanation on offer for the gap.

Averaged across all firms, no reduction in electricity consumption or total energy spending was detectable one year after the grants, and there was no detectable effect on revenue, profits or employment. Savings did appear for particular groups: electricity consumption fell by about 10% among micro and small firms, and electricity spending by about 20% among firms in the second call. The practical lesson for programme design is to scale a fixed grant to firm size, or to target smaller firms, and to justify it on investment and management change rather than on short-run business performance.

Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia: Digitrans, digital adoption support for MSMEs

Bulgaria, Poland and SlovakiaThree-arm RCT629 MSMEs2024–2025

The problem

Small businesses in Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia show limited adoption of digital tools beyond what regulation or the financial sector requires. The 2022 Digital Economy and Society Index places all three countries in the bottom quartile of EU members for business digitalisation.

Current public support programmes in the region largely overlook this: MSMEs account for 98% of businesses and two-thirds of employment, yet most programmes still emphasise technology adoption and financial assistance, leaving the managerial and organisational capabilities firms need to make use of new tools unaddressed.

What was done

The World Bank designed Digitrans, a standardised support instrument tested across Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia, integrating a financial subsidy with technical assistance to address both resource and capability constraints.

The financial subsidy provides up to EUR 2,000 per firm towards the cost of adopting digital business tools. The technical assistance component adds a 10-week programme of diagnostic assessment, group training and one-to-one advisory support.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Does pairing technical assistance with a financial subsidy improve MSME digitalisation outcomes more than a subsidy alone?”

The programme

The programme
A financial subsidy of up to EUR 2,000 per firm, with a subset of firms also receiving a 10-week technical assistance programme
Where
Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia; World Bank Digitrans pilot, EU-funded
Who is randomised
629 MSMEs with 5–35 employees and at least 12 months trading, drawn from over 1,600 applications
The design
Three-arm RCT, stratified by country, firm size and digital sophistication: comparison (209), subsidy only (210), subsidy plus technical assistance (210)
Outcomes tracked
Digital tool adoption and usage, managerial and organisational change, firm performance
Starting point
Small businesses show limited digital adoption beyond what regulation or banks require

Key steps and dates

ApplicationsMay–Jul 2024
→
Eligibility and interview
→
Random assignment
→
Subsidy and supportQ3–Q4 2024
→
Follow-up surveyFrom Q4 2024

How the programme was tested

Firms applied through an open call across Bulgaria, Poland and Slovakia in 2024, and those meeting the eligibility criteria completed a baseline interview. Eligible firms were then randomly assigned by the World Bank into one of three groups, with randomisation stratified within each country by firm size and digital sophistication so that groups were balanced from the outset.

Why this design. Strength: a three-arm design separates the effect of money from the effect of capability-building support, rather than only comparing supported firms to unsupported ones. Limitation: it needs a larger sample than a simple two-arm trial to detect the same effect size, since firms are split three ways.

Allocation resulted in balanced groups across a broad range of firm characteristics, creating a solid foundation for rigorous impact evaluation. There was no evidence of systematic differences between groups at baseline, so later changes can be reliably attributed to the support received rather than to pre-existing differences.

What the programme achieved

×2.5

Digital tool usage is 2.5 times higher with combined support

Firms that received both types of support increased their digital tool usage intensity more than twice as much as those with a subsidy alone.

×2

Managerial changes are more common with technical assistance

Firms in the combined support group were twice as likely to introduce internal organisational and managerial changes.

82% vs 67%

Combined support leads to deeper use of digital tools

Firms with technical assistance made better use of advanced software functions: 82% used CRM systems for client communication, against 67% in the subsidy-only group.

Firms receiving only a financial subsidy typically limited their spending to the subsidy value, with no meaningful increase in the sophistication of tools available to them. Effective digitalisation support needs to go beyond subsidising technology purchases: the benefits of combined support were especially strong among smaller firms, firms with low initial digital capabilities, service-sector firms and women-led businesses.

Bulgaria: human-led versus AI-led business consulting

Northern BulgariaFour-arm RCT600 firms2026–2027 (planned)

The problem

Northern Bulgaria’s micro and small enterprises (MSEs) - over 98% of firms in the region - lag far behind southern Bulgaria and the EU average. Depopulation and ageing have left “ghost towns” with shrinking labour pools and declining customer bases, and managerial skills, often picked up informally, limit the uptake of modern business practices. AI adoption is strikingly low: just 6.5% of firms in the region use AI, against an EU average of 13.5%.

What is being tested

The Bulgarian Small and Medium Enterprises Promotion Agency (BSMEPA) is launching a programme that pairs group training in managerial skills with one of three ways of delivering further support: one-to-one human-led consulting, a subscription to an off-the-shelf AI platform such as ChatGPT Premium, or a customised AI tool that proactively nudges managers towards it. The aim is to find out whether AI-led alternatives can match costly human consulting at a fraction of the price.

What the evaluation aims to answer

“Can lower-cost, AI-led consulting match the effectiveness of traditional human-led consulting for small firms?”

The programme

The programme
Group training in managerial skills, then one of three support modes: human-led consulting, an AI subscription, or a customised AI-led consulting tool
Where
Northern Bulgaria; Bulgarian Small and Medium Enterprises Promotion Agency (BSMEPA)
Who is randomised
600 MSEs (fewer than 50 employees) in retail, agricultural processing, services and manufacturing, stratified by sector, region and owner gender
The design
Four-arm RCT: pure control, training + human consulting, training + AI subscription, training + AI-led consulting tool (150 firms per arm)
Outcomes tracked
Firm sales, sales per worker, profits and exports; management practices, foreign-market knowledge, access to suppliers, finance and customers
Starting point
MSEs report 88% lower sales per worker than larger firms on average, and only 6.5% of regional firms use AI, against an EU average of 13.5%

Key steps and dates

Recruitment and applicationsBaseline Jan–Mar 2026 (planned)
→
Random assignment
→
Training and consulting rolloutMar–Sep 2026 (planned)
→
Endline surveyOct–Dec 2026 (planned)
→
Follow-up surveyOct–Dec 2027 (planned)

How the programme is designed

The Agency will mobilise applicants directly across northern Bulgaria; up to 450 eligible firms will be offered business support, with no applicant guaranteed a place. Once 600 eligible firms have applied, firms are randomly allocated, stratified by sector, region and owner gender, to a pure control group or one of three treatment arms.

Why this design. Strength: putting human-led consulting and two AI-based alternatives in the same trial, against the same control group, lets the evaluation rank all three by cost-effectiveness rather than comparing each in isolation. Limitation: with 150 firms per arm, the study is powered to detect a change in sales of around 26% or more, so more modest effects could go undetected.

What the evaluation will answer

Human-led vs AI-led

Whether a customised, proactively nudging AI tool can approach the effectiveness of costly one-to-one human consulting, at a lower marginal cost.

Off-the-shelf vs custom AI

Whether simply subscribing managers to an existing tool like ChatGPT Premium is enough, or whether a tailored, proactive AI platform is needed to change business practices.

How durable are the gains?

Whether improvements from AI-enabled consulting persist for longer than from human-led consulting, since managers keep the ability to self-serve after the programme ends.

The main risks to the evaluation are insufficient take-up of consulting or AI services, and non-random attrition between the baseline and the two follow-up rounds. The team is mitigating these with in-person mobilisation to build the applicant pool, and by collecting detailed contact and backup information from every participant to support tracking through autumn 2027.

Poland: AI assistance for smarter Horizon Europe applications

Poland, national programmeThree nested RCTs~120,000 SMEs reached

The problem

Polish SMEs underperform in Horizon Europe, the EU’s flagship innovation funding programme: a 16.5% success rate, against an EU-27 average of 20.3%. Funding is also highly concentrated - just 11 SMEs account for roughly a third of all Polish projects. A previous support scheme, “Grants for Eurogrants”, converted only 400 of about 1,700 applications into signed contracts, and just 19 firms ultimately secured EU funding. Only 20.1% of firms report knowing PARP, the agency responsible for this support.

What is being tested

The Polish Agency for Enterprise Development (PARP) is testing a three-stage pilot: behavioural messaging to raise awareness of its support; a machine-learning model that scores firms’ likelihood of Horizon Europe success, used to target encouragement calls; and an AI-based advisory tool paired with a human “technology scout” for firms that express interest in applying.

What the evaluation aims to answer

“Can behavioural messaging, algorithmic targeting and AI-assisted advice raise the number and quality of Polish Horizon Europe applications?”

The programme

The programme
A three-stage pilot: behavioural outreach messaging, machine-learning targeting with encouragement calls, and AI-tool-plus-technology-scout application support
Where
Poland; Polish Agency for Enterprise Development (PARP)
Who is randomised
~120,000 SMEs reached with outreach messages; ~11,000 Horizon-eligible firms in the targeting stage; up to ~500 interested firms in the support stage
The design
Three nested RCTs: a messaging A/B/C test, ranked-vs-random encouragement calls, and AI-tool-and-scout support (~200 firms) vs. a waitlist
Outcomes tracked
Email/ad engagement, applications to PARP and Horizon Europe, blind-rated application quality, funding success, and firm employment, revenue and survival
Starting point
Just 16.5% of Polish Horizon Europe applications succeed, against an EU-27 average of 20.3%, and only 20.1% of firms report knowing PARP

Key steps and dates

→

→

→

How the programme is designed

Three experiments are nested inside PARP’s own application funnel. First, roughly 120,000 SMEs are randomly shown one of three outreach messages, from a plain fact-based announcement to versions adding behavioural nudges and information about the new AI-powered support. Second, from a pool of about 11,000 Horizon-eligible firms, one half is ranked by a machine-learning model trained on past Horizon winners; the top 500 ranked firms and 500 randomly selected firms then both receive an encouragement call. Third, among the roughly 400–500 firms that register interest in extra support, 200 are randomly selected for immediate access to an AI advisory tool and a technology scout, while the remainder are waitlisted.

Why this design. Strength: nesting three experiments inside PARP’s own application funnel means each stage’s estimate is drawn from firms who arrive naturally through the programme, not an artificial sample. Limitation: because later stages depend on take-up from earlier ones, the sample shrinks at each step, leaving less statistical power for the innermost support experiment.

What the evaluation will answer

Does messaging move firms?

Whether behavioural nudges - social proof, urgency framing, expected-value framing - raise click-throughs and registrations more than PARP’s standard, fact-based messaging.

Can an algorithm find promising firms?

Whether a machine-learning “Horizon potential score”, trained on past winners, identifies firms more likely to submit competitive applications than outreach targeted at random.

Does AI-assisted support raise quality?

Whether pairing an AI advisory tool with a human technology scout increases application rates, application quality and ultimately funding success, compared with PARP’s standard waitlist resources.

The clearest risk to implementation is legal: whether informing firms that a machine-learning model identified them as “high-potential” is compatible with EU data-protection law is still under review, and may limit how the targeting stage can be communicated. The team is also tracking whether firms treat encouragement phone calls as credible outreach from PARP rather than as spam.

Romania (North-East): AI assistance for smarter grant reviews

North-East region, RomaniaMulti-phase RCT~800 proposals2026–2027 (planned)

The problem

At Romania’s Regional Development Agencies, grant application reviews are slow and inconsistent. The 2023 Microenterprise call at the North-East RDA took roughly seven months from submission to contract, generated 50 technical-financial appeals and 19 contracting appeals, and disadvantaged smaller firms: enterprises under two years old, with no employees or turnover below RON 250,000, succeeded only 14% of the time, against over 90% for firms with turnover of RON 1.5–2 million. Many applicants turn to a large consultancy industry - an estimated EUR 527 million in annual turnover across 1,500 firms - just to navigate the process.

What is being tested

The North-East Regional Development Agency (RDA), with the automation company UiPath, has built an AI decision-support tool that benchmarks proposed budgets, flags risks, and supports reviewers on eligibility and quality criteria. It is being embedded in the 2026 Microenterprise Growth Grant Call (about EUR 30 million), with reviewers randomly assigned to work with or without the tool, and funded projects randomly assigned to AI-assisted or standard monitoring.

What the evaluation aims to answer

“Can an AI decision-support tool make public grant reviews faster, more consistent and better targeted, without compromising fairness?”

The programme

The programme
An AI decision-support tool, co-developed with UiPath, that benchmarks budgets, flags risks and supports eligibility and quality scoring for grant reviewers
Where
North-East Romania; Regional Development Agency (RDA), embedded in the 2026 Microenterprise Growth Grant Call (~EUR 30 million)
Who is randomised
~1,000 expressions of interest and ~800 full proposals, reviewed by 30–40 trained reviewers; ~150 funded projects
The design
Multi-phase RCT: reviewers randomised to AI-assisted or human-only review at each stage; ~150 grants allocated by lottery within score bands; funded projects randomised to AI-assisted or standard monitoring
Outcomes tracked
Review time and scores, clarification requests and appeals, on-time disbursement, and funded-firm survival, employment, revenue and investment
Starting point
The 2023 call took roughly seven months from submission to contract; firms under two years old or with no employees had a 14% success rate, against over 90% for the highest-turnover firms

Key steps and dates

→
AI-assisted or human review
→
Full proposal and review
→

→
AI-assisted or standard monitoringUntil Q2 2027 (planned)

How the programme is designed

Around 1,000 expressions of interest are expected, each assigned to one of 30–40 trained reviewers who are randomly given, or not given, access to the AI tool; reviewers cycle between AI-assisted and unassisted batches so the comparison is not confounded by reviewer ability. Roughly 800 full proposals then go through the same randomised review process. Of the approximately 600 proposals that clear eligibility checks, about 150 are funded through a lottery run separately within AI-assisted and human-only score bands, so funding decisions stay comparable across review modes. Funded projects are then randomly assigned to AI-assisted or standard monitoring during implementation.

Why this design. Strength: randomising AI access at the reviewer-proposal level, with each reviewer cycling between AI-assisted and unassisted batches, isolates the effect of the tool from differences between reviewers. Limitation: because a reviewer with AI access can choose how much to rely on it, the design measures the effect of being offered AI support (intention-to-treat), not of using it, though usage telemetry will help distinguish the two.

What the evaluation will answer

Faster, more consistent reviews

Whether AI-assisted reviewers complete assessments faster, and with fewer clarification requests or appeals, than reviewers working without AI support.

Sharper targeting of promising firms

Whether the relationship between evaluation scores and firms’ later performance is stronger when those scores were produced with AI assistance.

Fair treatment across firm types

Whether AI assistance changes the pattern of scores and funding by owner gender, firm age and sector, as part of a pre-registered bias audit.

The central safeguard against biased AI outputs is a systematic audit of scoring patterns across firm groups after the expression-of-interest phase; if the audit finds significant disparities, the underlying prompts and models will be reviewed and adjusted before the full-proposal stage goes ahead.

Romania (South-Muntenia): the returns to innovation subsidies

South-Muntenia region, RomaniaTwo-stage cluster RCT~900 SMEs2026–2028 (planned)

The problem

South-Muntenia is a lagging EU region, ranked 243rd of EU regions on the Regional Innovation Scoreboard, with an Innovation Readiness Index of just 28. Only 6.3% of firms are classified as innovative, and just 1.4% of the workforce works in high-tech sectors. Between 2014 and 2020, Romania allocated EUR 1.6 billion to public research institutes but only EUR 74 million to private firms, and the region hosts just 3% of the country’s research infrastructure.

What is being tested

The South-Muntenia Regional Development Agency (SM-RDA) is allocating EUR 24.8 million in innovation subsidies to SMEs, cross-randomised with a management-training programme on change and innovation culture. Grants are assigned through a two-stage design: geographic or sectoral clusters are randomly assigned different shares of funded firms, and firms within each cluster are then randomly selected to receive funding - allowing the evaluation to separate a firm’s own private return from spillovers onto its neighbours.

What the evaluation aims to answer

“Do the private returns to innovation subsidies for SMEs exceed their social returns, once spillovers to other firms are taken into account?”

The programme

The programme
Innovation subsidy grants (50–70% co-financed by firms) for R&D projects, cross-randomised with a management-training programme on change and innovation culture
Where
South-Muntenia, Romania; South-Muntenia Regional Development Agency (SM-RDA), EUR 24.8 million SME innovation grant allocation
Who is randomised
~900 SMEs across 100 geographic or sectoral clusters (~9 firms per cluster), excluding agricultural firms
The design
Two-stage RCT: clusters randomised to 0%, 33%, 66% or 100% funding intensity, then firms randomised to funding within each cluster; management training cross-randomised at firm level
Outcomes tracked
Short-run revenue, inputs, profit, productivity, investment, pricing and product variety; medium- to long-run technology adoption, new products, licensing and patenting
Starting point
Only 6.3% of firms in the region are classified as innovative and just 1.4% of the workforce works in high-tech sectors, with an Innovation Readiness Index of 28

Key steps and dates

Call for proposalsJun–Sep 2026 (planned)
→
Cluster formation
→
Cluster-level intensityOct 2026 (planned)
→
Firm-level lotteryOct 2026 (planned)
→
Grant and monitoringTo Q1 2028; endline Q1–Q2 2028 (planned)

How the programme is designed

After the call for proposals closes, applicant firms are grouped into roughly 100 clusters based on geographic or sectoral proximity. Each cluster is then randomly assigned a funding intensity - the share of its firms that will receive a subsidy, set at 0%, 33%, 66% or 100% - and firms within the cluster are randomly selected for funding up to that share. Comparing outcomes across clusters with different intensities isolates spillover effects, while comparing funded and unfunded firms within the same cluster isolates each firm’s own private return.

Why this design. Strength: varying funding intensity across clusters, on top of randomising which firms within a cluster are funded, lets the evaluation separate a firm’s private return from spillovers onto its neighbours and competitors. Limitation: the design captures spillovers within a cluster but not across cluster boundaries, which the team plans to approximate separately with a structural, network-based model.

What the evaluation will answer

Do subsidies pay off for the firm?

Whether receiving an innovation subsidy raises a firm’s revenue, profits, productivity and investment in the short run, and its technology adoption and patenting over the longer run.

Do the gains spread, or just shift?

Whether firms in higher-intensity clusters see larger productivity gains through knowledge diffusion, or whether funded firms instead grow at competitors’ expense.

Does training make subsidies work harder?

Whether pairing the grant with training on change management and innovation culture strengthens firms’ ability to absorb and use the funding.

The main risk to the spillover analysis is statistical power: if spillovers turn out to be smaller than about 8 percentage points (roughly a third of the expected direct subsidy effect), that part of the analysis will be underpowered. Even so, the team notes the resulting estimates will still provide useful benchmarks for calibrating models of innovation diffusion, an area where experimental evidence remains scarce.

Colombia: scaling up management consulting

Antioquia, Cundinamarca, Valle del Cauca and Eje CafeteroThree-arm RCT159 firms2012–2018

The problem

Colombian auto parts manufacturers show wide variation in management practices, and weaker practices are closely tied to lower productivity. Structured, hands-on management consulting can improve how firms are run, but delivering it in a resource-intensive, one-to-one format is costly - a cost that can be prohibitive for small and medium firms to finance on their own, and for governments seeking to support large numbers of firms at once.

What was done

The World Bank, in partnership with the Government of Colombia, tested two lower-cost ways to deliver management consulting to small and medium manufacturers: individual, firm-by-firm consulting delivered by local teams, and a more novel group-based model that works with several firms together to bring costs down further.

Every participating firm, including the comparison group, first received a diagnostic assessment of its management practices across five areas - logistics, human resources, finance, marketing and sales, and production - carried out by a team of specialist consultants.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Can lower-cost individual or group-based consulting improve small and medium firms’ management practices and performance?”

The programme

The programme
A diagnostic for every firm, followed by individual or group-based management consulting
Where
Colombia; auto parts manufacturing sector
Who is randomised
159 eligible firms (manufacturers, tier 1/2 suppliers, 2+ years old, 10+ workers), in 53 matched triplets
The design
Three-arm RCT: comparison, individual consulting (~USD 28,950/firm), group consulting (~USD 10,500/firm)
Outcomes tracked
Management practices; employment, sales, value-added and profits via administrative records
Starting point
Baseline management scores ranged from 43 to 51 out of 100 across practice areas

Key steps and dates

ApplicationsAnnounced Apr 2012
→
Diagnostic phaseJun–Oct 2013
→
Matched-triplet lotteryNov 2013
→
Consulting deliveredIndividual Mar–Nov 2014; group Sep 2015–May 2016
→
Outcome tracking2014–2018

How the programme was tested

218 firms applied to Colombia’s Programa de Extensionismo Tecnológico. After screening for eligibility - established auto parts manufacturers that were tier 1 or 2 suppliers, at least two years old, with at least ten employees, and located in Antioquia, Cundinamarca, Valle del Cauca or the Eje Cafetero - 159 firms formed the final sample.

Firms were then grouped into 53 matched triplets, formed to minimise differences in location, size, labour productivity and management practices between the three firms in each triplet. Within each triplet, firms were randomly allocated to a comparison group, individual consulting, or group consulting.

Why this design. Strength: matching firms into triplets before randomising keeps the three groups closely balanced on size, productivity and starting management practices, even with a modest overall sample. Limitation: with only 53 firms in each arm, the design has less statistical power to detect small effects than a larger, simple two-arm trial.

What the programme achieved

+9 pp

Management practices improved in both groups

Individual and group consulting each raised firms’ management practice scores by around 9 percentage points on average, relative to a baseline of 56%.

+28% to +33%

Sales and profits rose for group-consulting firms

Firms receiving group-based consulting saw significant gains in sales, value-added and profits; the individual programme’s effects on firm performance were smaller and not statistically significant.

USD 273,000

Estimated average annual profit increase, group consulting

At a cost of about USD 10,500 per firm, the estimated profit gains from group consulting paid back the programme’s cost within about a month.

Group-consulting firms reported far more contact with each other after the programme than before it: business links between study firms were almost non-existent at baseline, but around half of group participants said they still communicated with other group members roughly a year later, and firms whose management practices changed the most tended to be grouped with others who also improved the most - evidence that peer learning, not just lower delivery cost, contributed to the group programme’s stronger results on firm performance.

The group programme’s rollout was delayed by more than a year due to a budgetary payment delay, and only 68% of group-assigned firms completed the intervention, compared with 87% for the individually delivered programme. Delays like this are a practical risk to weigh when comparing the cost-effectiveness of different delivery models.

Yemen: testing whether internships help youth find work

Sanaa and Aden, YemenStratified RCT583 young graduates2013–2015

The problem

Young jobseekers in Yemen often face a catch-22: firms are reluctant to hire someone without prior work experience, yet that experience is hard to gain without first being hired. Early jobs matter because they build both job-specific and soft skills, but firms may under-invest in providing them - a reluctance that can be sharper still in a fragile, conflict-affected setting. Job opportunities for young graduates were also increasingly scarce following Yemen’s 2011–12 political crisis.

What was done

The Enterprise Revitalization and Employment Pilot (EREP) tested a subsidised-internship model: the programme paid half the cost of a six-month internship, with the host firm covering the rest, and matched applicants to firms based on the firm’s stated requirements. Recent graduates in Sanaa and Aden applied online and were screened for eligibility before being considered.

What experimentation sought to answer

“Can a subsidised matchmaking programme help young jobseekers gain their first work experience, and does that experience translate into better employment outcomes afterwards?”

The programme

The programme
Enterprise Revitalization and Employment Pilot (EREP), internship component: matching grants covering half the cost of a six-month internship, plus matchmaking between graduates and firms
Where
Republic of Yemen; Sanaa and Aden
Who is randomised
583 eligible youth (Yemeni, Sanaa/Aden residents, graduated within 2 years), to treatment (430) or control (153)
The design
Stratified RCT: treatment group offered matchmaking to internships subsidised at 50% of cost, for up to six months
Outcomes tracked
Employment, months worked and earnings, via a follow-up phone survey
Starting point
3,487 eligible youth applied; 500 were randomly invited to a two-day basic skills training (477 attended)

Key steps and dates

→

→

How the programme was tested

The programme launched in August 2013 through a project website, media events and campus outreach. Applications ran from October to December 2013, closing with 3,487 eligible applicants. Of these, 500 were randomly invited to a two-day basic skills training - chosen with probability proportional to the demand for their specialisation - and 477 attended.

The resulting experimental sample of 583 individuals was randomly assigned, within strata defined by specialisation and city, to a treatment group of 430 (offered matchmaking to a subsidised internship) or a control group of 153. Eleven human resource advisors then matched treatment-group applicants to firms based on the firm’s requirements, sending CVs for firms to interview and decide whether to offer a placement.

Why this design. Strength: randomising within strata defined by specialisation and city, among applicants who had already been invited to training, helps ensure the treatment and control groups started out comparable in skills and local job-market conditions. Limitation: more applicants were assigned to treatment than there were internship placements available, so actual take-up (36.7%) fell well below the treatment assignment rate - which is why the study reports both an intention-to-treat effect and a larger effect scaled up for those who actually took up an internship.

What the programme achieved

+17 pp / +42 pp

More likely to have worked at all in 2014

Being offered internship matchmaking raised the chance of having worked at all during 2014 by 17 percentage points on an intention-to-treat basis, from a 61% employment rate in the control group, and by 42 percentage points among those who actually took up an internship.

+0.35 s.d. / +0.85 s.d.

Employment gains persisted months after the internship ended

On a standardised index of employment outcomes measured three to five months after internships ended, effects remained statistically significant: 0.35 standard deviations on an intention-to-treat basis, and 0.85 standard deviations for those who took up an internship.

36.7%

Take-up among those assigned to treatment

Only 158 of the 430 people assigned to treatment were actually matched to and completed an internship placement, reflecting how binding capacity constraints were relative to demand for internship positions.

The internship raised employment more for male applicants than for female applicants, so the programme did not close the pre-existing gender gap in employment outcomes. Because the evaluation covers only one year of the programme rather than the two originally planned, the study had limited statistical power to say precisely how much smaller the effect was for women.

Conflict escalated partway through the programme: Houthi forces took control of Sanaa in September 2014, and the second year of the pilot was cancelled, roughly halving the intended sample and reducing statistical power. The follow-up survey was also fielded earlier and more narrowly than planned, amid displacement and shortages of fuel, electricity and water. It also remains possible that some of the employment gains for treated youth came partly at the expense of other jobseekers in the economy - a general equilibrium effect this study could not measure.
