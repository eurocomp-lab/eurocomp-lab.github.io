Step 6: identify available data sources

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page covers the choice between primary and secondary data, how to assess what administrative sources already exist and whether they cover the right indicators, where to find secondary (firm-level) data, and how to set up data-sharing agreements.

With the Theory of Change (ToC), learning questions and indicators in place, the feasibility of the randomised controlled trial (RCT) confirmed and the randomisation designed, the next question is where the data will come from. This is a strategic decision that must be made before the intervention launches, not an operational detail to be worked out later. Getting it wrong wastes resources, delays the evaluation, or produces data that cannot support the intended analysis.

The fundamental decision: primary, secondary, or both?

For each indicator in your evaluation framework, you must decide whether to rely on primary data collected specifically for the evaluation, secondary data that already exists in administrative records, or a combination of both.

Primary data is information collected directly from target units (e.g. firms, individuals, organisations) through surveys or interviews. It gives you control over exactly what is measured and can capture results and mechanisms that administrative records do not contain. The trade-off is cost, time, and respondent burden.

Secondary data draws on records that already exist: censuses on the household level, national business registries, tax authority data, social security and employment records, customs records, patent office databases, or the intervention’s own project management system on the firm level. It is generally cheaper, covers larger samples, and often provides years of pre-intervention history that is invaluable for establishing baselines. The trade-off is that it may not contain the specific indicators you would like to measure, and access can take time to negotiate.

Where administrative data sources are well established and reliable, as is generally the case for firm-level administrative data (e.g. national business registries) across the European Union, combining both sources is often the most effective approach. Secondary data provides broad coverage and pre-intervention history, while primary survey data captures the results and mechanisms that administrative records cannot.

North‑East Romania: data sources

North-East Romania used a combination of primary and secondary data: self-reported survey data was collected using a structured questionnaire that complements administrative data sources.

Assess what you already have

Before commissioning a new survey, take stock of the administrative data you already hold or can access. Project management systems typically record beneficiary characteristics, grant amounts, payment dates, and reported output indicators. For firms, national statistical offices, tax authorities, business registries, and customs agencies hold data on turnover, employment, exports, costs, expenditures and tax payments, often going back many years, which is particularly valuable for establishing pre-intervention baselines. For people, social security, public employment service and education records can play the same role.

Two questions determine whether administrative sources can be useful for an evaluation.

First, do they cover the right indicators? Administrative records capture variables that may or may not align with the results the evaluation is designed to measure. An innovation intervention that wants to measure research and development (R&D) expenditure or product novelty will find that some business registries simply do not record these. Before relying on administrative data, map each indicator in your evaluation framework against what each source actually contains, and identify the gaps.

Second, can the administrative data be linked to the participants in the evaluation? If official identification numbers are collected at application stage, they can serve as a linking key to match intervention records against tax, registry, social security or customs data. For firms, that means the VAT or national registration number; for people, the national identification number, where national law allows its use. This makes it possible to measure results without any additional burden on participants. Where no common identifier exists, linkage may still be possible through name and address matching, but this requires more effort and introduces matching errors.

Data-sharing agreements

Access to administrative data rarely happens automatically. Relevant microdata are typically held by separate government bodies and subject to their own access rules. Negotiating access takes time (in some countries, many months) and must be initiated well before the evaluation begins.

Preparing the request.

  • Send an official letter. An official request from the managing authority, signed at an appropriate level of seniority, often carries more weight with a data-holding body than informal contact. It also creates a record of the request.
  • Be specific about which data you need. List the variables, the units (for example, all firms in the evaluation sample), the years, and the level of detail. Requests for “all available data” are hard to approve and tend to be slowed down or refused.
  • Explain why you need it. Link each requested item to an indicator in your Theory of Change and to the evaluation question it helps answer. This shows that the request is limited to what is necessary and helps the data holder justify its decision.
  • Plan for several rounds of negotiation. Expect questions, requests for clarification, and revisions to the scope, the access conditions, or the legal basis. Build this time into the evaluation timeline, and involve your Data Protection Officer and, where helpful, legal advisers from the start.

What the agreement should cover. A data-sharing agreement between the managing authority and the data-holding body sets out the rights and responsibilities of each side. Good practice is to include the following, with the technical details in annexes that can be updated without rewriting the agreement:

  • Parties and roles. Who provides the data, who receives it, and who is responsible for decisions on how it is used. Name a contact person on each side.
  • Purpose and legal basis. The specific evaluation purpose for which the data may be used, and the legal basis for sharing it. Where access relies on the consent of participants, the agreement should refer to it and state that signed consent forms are available on request. The data should not be used for any other purpose without written agreement.
  • Description of the data. The datasets, variables, time period, and units covered, the format, and the identifiers used to link records (for example, a tax or registration number). Specify whether the data is pseudonymised or anonymised.
  • Access arrangements. How the data will be provided: transferred to the evaluation team, or accessed in a secure environment. State how often it will be updated, which matters if you need data for several years after the intervention.
  • Authorised users and confidentiality. Only named members of the evaluation team may access identifiable data, on a need-to-know basis, and all of them are bound by confidentiality. Re-identifying individuals or firms, and linking the data to other sources for unrelated purposes, should be prohibited.
  • Data security. Minimum safeguards such as encryption, pseudonymisation, multi-factor authentication, restricted access with access logs, secure storage, and regular backups. The data holder may ask for the right to verify that these safeguards are in place.
  • Third parties and transfers. Whether consultants, data collection firms, or research partners may receive the data. This should require prior approval by the data holder and the same obligations as the original recipient. Any transfer outside the EU, for example to an international research partner, should be stated and covered by an appropriate legal mechanism.
  • Requests from individuals. What happens if a person or firm asks to see, correct, or delete their data: who is notified, and how quickly.
  • Security incidents. The recipient must notify the data holder promptly, within a defined time limit, of any loss, unauthorised access, or disclosure, and cooperate in the response.
  • Duration, retention, and deletion. The period for which the agreement and access are valid, which should cover the full evaluation including follow-up rounds. At the end, the data must be returned or securely destroyed, with written confirmation. Confidentiality and security obligations should continue after the agreement ends.
  • Publication and outputs. Results must be published only in aggregate or anonymised form. The data holder may ask to review outputs before publication, to check that no unit can be identified.
  • Termination and changes. The conditions under which either side may end the agreement, for example in the event of a breach, and how amendments are agreed in writing.

General Data Protection Regulation compliance must be addressed explicitly in the agreement. Draft the agreement together with the data holder’s own legal and data protection teams, as many institutions have their own templates and requirements.

Link to informed consent. The data-sharing agreement and participants’ consent are two separate but complementary requirements. The agreement is between the managing authority and the data-holding body. The consent, collected from participating units at the point of application, is what allows the managing authority to request their records from that body. Data holders will often want to see evidence of this consent before releasing any data. For this reason:

  • Align the scope of the agreement with the scope of the consent. The agreement should not cover more data, years, or purposes than participants have agreed to.
  • Make sure the agreement is valid for as long as the consent, which should itself cover all planned follow-up rounds.
  • Agree in advance what happens when a participant withdraws consent: how the managing authority informs the data holder, and how that firm’s records are excluded from future transfers and deleted where required.
