Integrating randomisation into national legislation

Summary

This page covers the legal preconditions for using randomisation under EU Cohesion Policy, the key elements any national legal framework must address, and country examples from Spain and France showing how RCTs have been embedded in law.

For a Managing Authority to use randomisation as part of its beneficiary selection process, the legal framework governing that programme must either explicitly permit it or be flexible enough to accommodate it without ambiguity. Most national frameworks were designed around two conventional selection methods - ranking proposals by score, or first-come-first-served - and say nothing about random assignment. A clear legal basis is the precondition for experimental evaluation to happen at all. DG Regio provided formal confirmation that randomisation is permissible under Euorpean Union (EU) Cohesion Policy rules. The key regulatory anchor is Article 44 of the Common Provisions Regulation (EU) 2021/1060, which requires Managing Authorities to evaluate the effectiveness of their programmes but does not prescribe the method - creating space for RCTs as the most rigorous option available.

Key elements any legal framework must address

Regardless of the legislative level - national law, implementing regulation, or programme-specific instrument - the following elements should be covered.

  • Explicit permission to randomise, with a two-stage process. Randomisation must be framed as applying after eligibility assessment, not instead of it. Stage 1 assesses all applicants against pre-defined competence-based criteria. Stage 2 randomly assigns equally eligible applicants to treatment and control groups. This makes clear that the lottery does not replace merit-based selection - it determines who among equally deserving applicants receives support when the programme is oversubscribed.
  • Conditions for use. The legal text should specify when randomisation is permissible: there must be a clear research question, measurable outcomes, genuine oversubscription, a sufficient sample size confirmed by a power analysis, a pre-registered evaluation protocol, and informed consent from all applicants at the point of application.
  • Transparency and documentation. The randomisation algorithm, software, and results must be documented, stored, and publicly communicated. Notarial certification of the draw is recommended. All applicants - selected and non-selected - must be informed of the outcome and the reason for it.
  • Protection of the control group. Non-selected eligible applicants must retain the right to apply to future calls. Providing equivalent support to control group members through parallel instruments during the evaluation period should be avoided wherever possible, to prevent contamination of the results.
  • Role of expert panels. Expert evaluation panels assess eligibility in Stage 1. Their role ends once the eligible pool is defined. The randomised algorithm determines selection from that point - panels do not rank or choose among eligible applicants.
  • Data access and independence. The evaluation must be conducted by an independent team. Data-sharing agreements with administrative data holders (tax authorities, business registries) must be in place before the evaluation begins, with GDPR compliance addressed explicitly.

Country examples

Spain: a royal decree as legal foundation

Spain provides the most developed example of embedding RCTs in a national legal framework for public funding. During the COVID-19 pandemic, the Spanish government established its Inclusion Policy Lab as part of the Minimum Income Scheme and Social Shield policy. The lab ran 32 RCTs with €200 million in funding, partnering with leading academic institutions including Nobel Laureate Abhijit Banerjee and J-PAL Europe. Evaluated interventions - including personalised employment services for the homeless and digital skills training - showed significant improvements in employment rates and life satisfaction. The legal foundation was provided by Royal Decree 378/2022 (Real Decreto 378/2022, de 17 de mayo), which formally integrated RCTs into the use of public funds under Spain’s Recovery, Transformation and Resilience Plan. The Decree outlined the responsibilities of subsidy recipients participating in pilot evaluations, mandated coordination with beneficiary entities, and promoted evidence-based policymaking, transparency, and accountability as explicit legal objectives.

Two factors were critical to the Spanish success. First, the initiative was framed as a social investment rather than an administrative requirement - this framing secured the political backing needed to pass the legislation and maintain it through a change of government. Second, when the May 2023 elections brought a new administration, a Memorandum of Understanding signed between the World Bank and the regional government of Extremadura prior to the elections ensured continuity in the evaluation process. The MoU covered technical advice on evaluation design, scoring mechanisms, randomisation of applicant selection, and independent outsourcing of the evaluation to prevent political influence.

France: constitutional amendment as enabling legislation

France adopted a higher-level approach, embedding the right to conduct policy experiments directly in the 2003 constitutional amendment, which added provisions under Articles 37-1 and 72 of the French Constitution allowing local and national authorities to run policy experiments. This constitutional foundation enabled 300 policy experiments by 2019, including the Youth Policy Lab (€150 million in public funding, supported by economists Esther Duflo and Marc Gurgand), which led directly to legislative reforms in education and health insurance.
