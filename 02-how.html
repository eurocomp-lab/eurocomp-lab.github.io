How to design a randomised controlled trial

Summary

This page covers the internal team to assemble before starting, and eight sequential steps for designing a credible randomised controlled trial (RCT): Theory of Change (ToC), learning questions, indicators, feasibility, randomisation, data sources, data collection, and data quality.

A well-designed RCT does not begin with randomisation. It begins with a clear understanding of what an intervention is trying to achieve and why. The eight steps below set out the preparatory work that makes randomisation meaningful and the results credible. They are numbered in a logical order, but their work overlaps in practice, and most of it is done before the first call for applications is launched.

Most examples on these pages involve firms, because the EuroCOMP Lab’s experiments have so far focused on business support. The same steps apply to any intervention, whether it reaches people, firms, schools, municipalities or other organisations.

The timeline below shows when the work of each step usually happens. Everything up to the random draw has to be in place before the intervention starts, which is what makes the comparison between the two groups valid.

Eight steps for designing an RCT successfully

Before you start: assemble your internal evaluation team

Identify who inside the managing authority will take on each role before working through the eight steps.

Step 1: develop a Theory of Change

Map the causal logic of your intervention, from inputs to long-term goals, and make the assumptions explicit.

Step 2: define learning questions

Formulate the specific questions the evaluation is designed to answer, grounded in the ToC.

Step 3: determine relevant indicators

Select the outcome indicators that will be used to measure change across each level of the ToC.

Tool: Indicator repository

Step 4: check whether an RCT is feasible

Check whether the conditions for a valid RCT are in place: oversubscription, timing, legal basis, and political buy-in.

Also in this step: Power calculations · Legal basis for random selection

Step 5: how to randomise

Choose a randomisation design, from a simple lottery to a phase-in or an encouragement design, decide how the draw is run, and pre-register the design before the intervention begins.

Also in this step: Balance checks

Step 6: identify available data sources

Decide whether to rely on primary data, administrative data, or a combination, and map what is already available.

Step 7: collect data

Design and implement the data collection: surveys, administrative data access, and quality controls.

Also in this step: Terms of reference · Informed consent

Step 8: ensure high-quality data

Put in place the checks needed to detect and address data quality problems before analysis begins.

Before you start: assemble your internal evaluation team

The managing authority brings knowledge that no outside expert can replicate: how the intervention works in practice, who the target beneficiaries are, what the political constraints are, and what a meaningful result would look like. External consultants and data collection firms are available to provide methodological and operational support, and many managing authorities will choose to work with them. But whether the RCT is implemented with external support or largely in-house, a core internal team needs to be in place from the start. Without it, key decisions get made by default rather than by design, and the evaluation risks being shaped by what is convenient rather than what matters.

Before working through the eight steps, identify who inside the managing authority will take on each of the following roles:

An evaluation focal point

The person who leads the process, coordinates across the other roles, and serves as the primary contact for the external evaluation team. Ideally someone with some familiarity with impact evaluation methods, or who has been through the EuroCOMP Lab training.

A project manager

Someone with detailed knowledge of the intervention: eligibility criteria, selection process, implementation timeline, and the population of applicants. This is the person who can answer the policy questions that drive the evaluation design, particularly what effect size would be meaningful and what the intervention is realistically expected to achieve.

Where possible, it is also helpful to involve:

A legal or procurement officer

Involved early, to confirm that the national legal framework permits randomisation and to manage the procurement process.

A data protection officer

To ensure that data-sharing agreements and General Data Protection Regulation compliance are in place before fieldwork begins.

The internal team does not need to be large: in smaller managing authorities, one or two people may cover several roles. What matters is that the responsibilities are clearly assigned and that the evaluation focal point has the authority to make decisions and the access to intervention information they need.

Where internal capacity or methodological expertise is limited, external experts can be hired to support evaluation design, data collection, or both.
