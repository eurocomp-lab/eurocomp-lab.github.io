Step 8: ensure high-quality data

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page covers quality controls before fieldwork, real-time monitoring during fieldwork, and post-collection steps: cleaning, balance checks, attrition assessment, and validation against administrative data.

Data quality is not something that can be fixed after the fact. Problems that go undetected during fieldwork (inconsistent answers, enumerator errors, item non-response, or systematic biases in who responds) cannot be fully corrected at the analysis stage. The purpose of this step is to put in place the checks that catch these problems early, while there is still time to act on them.

Quality assurance in a randomised controlled trial (RCT) operates at three levels: before fieldwork begins, during fieldwork, and after data collection is complete. Each level requires different tools and different responsibilities.

Before fieldwork: build quality in from the start

The most effective quality controls are the ones built into the data collection instrument and process before a single interview takes place.

Build validation rules into the digital form. Every digital survey should include:

  • mandatory fields for key variables
  • range checks, flagging implausible values such as negative employment or turnover greater than a plausible ceiling
  • logical consistency checks (for example, research and development (R&D) expenditure cannot exceed total costs)
  • skip logic that prevents respondents from reaching questions that do not apply to them

These rules catch errors at the point of entry, before they enter the dataset.

Pilot the questionnaire. As described in Step 7, no questionnaire should go to the field untested. The pilot should specifically assess whether questions produce consistent, interpretable answers, not just whether they are understood. If two respondents from similar firms give very different answers to the same question, the wording may be ambiguous even if both respondents felt they understood it.

Train enumerators thoroughly. For Computer-Assisted Telephone Interviews (CATI) and Computer-Assisted Personal Interviews (CAPI) surveys, enumerator quality is one of the strongest predictors of data quality. Training should cover not only how to administer each question but also how to handle refusals, clarify without leading, and record partial responses. Enumerators should complete mock interviews before fieldwork begins.

Pre-specify the analysis plan. Before any data is collected, the primary outcomes, the estimator, and the subgroup analyses should be publicly pre-registered. This prevents selective reporting (the practice of reporting only the results that look good) and provides a clear audit trail from design to findings. Pre-registration is covered under Step 5, but its data quality implications deserve emphasis here: it disciplines both data collection and analysis.

During fieldwork: monitor in real time

Once fieldwork begins, the evaluation team should monitor progress continuously rather than waiting for a complete dataset to arrive.

Track response rates by group. Response rates should be monitored separately for the treatment and control groups throughout fieldwork. If response rates diverge significantly (for example, if control firms are less willing to respond), this is an early warning of differential attrition that can bias results.

Conduct back-checks. A back-check involves re-contacting a random subset of completed interviews (typically 10%) to verify a selection of answers. Significant discrepancies between the original interview and the back-check indicate either enumerator error or data fabrication, both of which require immediate investigation. Back-checks are standard practice in telephone (CATI) and face-to-face (CAPI) surveys and should be a contractual requirement for any data collection firm.

Monitor enumerator performance. Each enumerator’s response patterns should be reviewed regularly for signs of data fabrication: unusually short interview durations, identical or near-identical responses across interviews, or suspiciously high completion rates. Any enumerator showing these patterns should be flagged and their recent interviews back-checked at a higher rate.

Flag implausible values immediately. The survey platform should be configured to generate automatic alerts for out-of-range values or logical inconsistencies. These should be reviewed by the field supervisor within 24 hours and, where possible, resolved by calling the respondent back.

After data collection: clean, document, and validate

Once fieldwork is complete, the dataset must be cleaned and validated before analysis begins. This process should be systematic and fully documented so that it can be reproduced and audited.

Check balance between treatment and control groups. The first analytical task after baseline data collection is to verify that randomisation produced comparable treatment and control groups. Run balance checks across all key baseline characteristics including firm size, age, sector, turnover, and the primary outcomes at baseline. A significant imbalance on any of these variables should be investigated: it may indicate a flaw in the randomisation procedure, or it may be a chance occurrence that needs to be controlled for in the analysis.

Document all cleaning decisions. Every decision made during data cleaning (how missing values are handled, how outliers are treated, how variables are recoded) must be recorded in a cleaning log. This log is part of the evaluation’s audit trail and should be shared with the managing authority alongside the final dataset.

Validate against external sources. Where possible, key variables from the primary survey should be cross-checked against administrative data. For example, self-reported employment figures can be compared against social security records; self-reported turnover can be compared against VAT declarations. Large discrepancies between survey and administrative data are a signal of measurement error that needs to be understood before it affects the analysis.

Assess and report attrition. Compare the characteristics of firms that completed the endline survey with those that did not. If firms that dropped out differ systematically from those that remained (in size, sector, or baseline outcome levels), attrition is selective and may bias the results. Report attrition rates and the results of these comparisons transparently in the evaluation report, and apply appropriate analytical corrections (such as inverse probability weighting) where differential attrition is detected.

A note on administrative data quality

The quality assurance steps above apply primarily to primary survey data. Administrative data comes with its own quality challenges that require a different approach.

Before using administrative data as an outcome source, the evaluation team should assess:

  • whether variable definitions have changed over the data period
  • whether there are systematic gaps in coverage (for example, firms below a VAT registration threshold)
  • whether reporting lags mean that the data will be available in time for the evaluation
  • whether the data has been used in comparable evaluations and, if so, what quality issues were encountered

Where administrative data quality is uncertain, the evaluation should include a validation exercise, comparing a subset of administrative records against survey-reported values from the same firms, before relying on it as the primary outcome source.
