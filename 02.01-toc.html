Step 1: develop a Theory of Change

Step 1Step 2Step 3Step 4Step 5Step 6Step 7Step 8

Summary

This page covers what a Theory of Change (ToC) is, how it relates to the intervention logic required in cohesion policy programmes, each component of the causal chain from inputs to goals with examples, and why building one strengthens both evaluation design and policy lessons.

Before any data is collected and before any evaluation contract is signed, a managing authority needs to be clear about what the intervention is trying to achieve, how progress towards that goal will be measured, and what data will be needed to do so. A ToC makes this logic explicit and evaluation-ready.

What is a ToC - and how does it relate to your intervention logic?

In cohesion policy programmes, managing authorities are already required to articulate an intervention logic - a structured account of how a policy instrument is expected to produce its intended results. A ToC builds on this familiar concept and makes it more explicit and evaluation-ready.

Where an intervention logic maps the pathway from inputs to outputs to results, a ToC adds two important elements: it specifies the assumptions that must hold at each step for the logic to work, and it makes clear which links in the chain are strong and which are uncertain. This is precisely the information a randomised controlled trial (RCT) is designed to test.

If your intervention already has a well-developed intervention logic in its document or partnership agreement, you have a strong starting point. The task of building a ToC is largely one of making that logic more precise - defining each step clearly enough that it can be measured.

Components of a ToC

A ToC follows a causal chain from resources invested to long-term change achieved, with assumptions sitting at every junction:

Inputs are the resources, efforts, and actions invested in a policy. For cohesion policy programmes, inputs might include co-financing from the European Union, national co-financing, and the administrative capacity of the managing authority and intermediate bodies.

North‑East Romania: inputs

  • Number of staff involved in designing and implementing the digitalisation grants
  • Available budget to disrupt digitalisation grants

Activities are the concrete actions undertaken to deliver the intervention. These are what the intervention actually does.

North‑East Romania: activities

  • Outreach and information campaign for potential applicants
  • Pre-assessment questionnaire to express interest in digitalisation grants
  • Call for proposals to deliver digitalisation grants

Outputs are represent the direct and immediate results of the activities conducted within a policy. They are tangible and observable products or deliverables that demonstrate the completion of specific tasks. For cohesion policy programmes, these correspond closely to the output indicators that managing authorities are already required to report to the European Commission.

North‑East Romania: outputs

  • Digitalisation grants are awarded to selected companies

Short-term outcomes refer to the immediate changes that occur as a direct result of the outputs. These outcomes are often related to changes in knowledge, attitudes, skills, and behaviours among the target population. Although short-term outcomes are not the intervention’s ultimate goals, they play a crucial role in achieving the larger objectives.

North‑East Romania: short-term outcomes

  • Digital solution procured, installed, configured and tested
  • Digital solution is operationalised, used by employees, customers and others in the supply chain.​​

Medium-term outcomes are the intermediate changes that occur due to sustained efforts and progress from short-term outcomes. These outcomes indicate progress towards the overall goal and often involve shifts in social norms, policies, or organisational practices. These correspond closely to the result indicators that cohesion policy programmes report on, though impact evaluations typically aim to go further by establishing a credible causal link between the intervention and the result.

North‑East Romania: medium-term outcomes

  • Companies achieve advanced digital transformation
  • Improved time management and business efficiency

Goals are a policy’s broad, long-term aspirations or objectives. They describe the ultimate impact or change that the policy aims to achieve. Goals are usually overarching and may require significant time and effort to accomplish.

North‑East Romania: goals

  • Increase in sales, employment, productivity (Total Factor Productivity) and profitability
  • Higher resilience to economic shocks, due to the business versatility ensured though the digital solutions

Assumptions are the underlying beliefs, conditions, or factors that are considered to be true but have not yet been fully proven or tested. Assumptions may involve external factors, stakeholder behaviour, or contextual influences that must align for the theory to work as expected.

North‑East Romania: assumptions

  • Employees will possess, or will be able to acquire, the digital skills needed to effectively use the solution for advanced digital transformation
  • Companies have sufficient organisational flexibility to change internal processes
  • Companies have the organisational capacity
  • Companies’ customers and suppliers are sufficiently active in digital channels (online payments, e-commerce transactions, digital invoicing) to make adoption beneficial
  • Suppliers, partners, and customers will engage with and use digital links (communications, transactions, data exchange) established by companies, ensuring network effects and value creation.

Why invest time in developing a ToC?

Developing a ToC is well worth the effort for two important reasons. First, it sharpens the design of your evaluation. By mapping out the causal logic of an intervention, a ToC generates clear research questions and helps teams identify which outcomes to measure and what data to collect. It can be built collaboratively - by starting from the desired final outcomes and working backwards through the chain of events needed to get there - and this process is particularly useful in an intervention partnership context, where multiple stakeholders may have different views on how the policy is supposed to work. It also prompts a credibility check: is there actually evidence, from comparable interventions in other regions or member states, to support each step in the logic?

Second, a ToC produces richer and more transferable policy lessons. Measuring the right intermediate outcomes reveals not just whether an intervention worked, but how and why it worked. Opening this “black box” transforms evaluation results from a simple pass/fail verdict into actionable insight that can inform the next programming period. The knowledge gained also travels better - findings grounded in a clear causal logic are more readily applied by other managing authorities facing similar challenges in different regional contexts.

North‑East Romania: why investing time

In Romania, the World Bank supported the managing authority in developing a ToC and in applying systematic methods to quantify the managing authority’s own expectations about intervention impacts before the call launched. This exercise revealed gaps between what the intervention was designed to achieve and what its indicators actually measured - allowing the design to be adjusted before implementation rather than after the evaluation.
